1. A randomized trial of 802 patients finds a pre-visit prep agent works better when it’s built from explicit sub-tasks, not an open chat window — and clinicians and patients judge the improvement differently.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 30 2026)

Google Health compared a structured agent — context-gathering, expectation-setting, question-list building, summary, each a distinct step — against an open, minimally-prompted chatbot on the same base model. Clinician-rated comprehensive preparation rose from 50.6% to 67.2% (P<0.05); patient confidence in knowing what to ask rose from 67.1% to 81.4% (P<0.01).

The two numbers don’t track each other — “clinician and participant metrics were not significantly correlated” — so one satisfaction score can’t stand in for both audiences. The design lesson: decomposing a prep/intake flow into mandatory sub-tasks beats trusting an open conversation to cover the same ground.

2. Replit found that AI-only security review gives different verdicts on functionally identical code depending on how it’s phrased — so they kept a deterministic static-analysis layer instead of trusting the AI reviewer alone.

(single-source, vendor-primary — Replit Blog, Oct 28 2026)

Replit’s own account treats this as a reason to design around AI judgment, not on top of it: proposing a change and approving it for production “must remain distinct” roles, and MicroVMs isolate each execution environment at the kernel level. Its productivity numbers are explicitly caveated as “internal experience, not a promised customer outcome.”

The decision worth stealing: treat an AI reviewer as one signal among several, never the sole gate, when the judge is non-deterministic on phrasing.

3. A 360-person study finds people send an AI assistant’s version of a message instead of what they actually meant to say — three-quarters of the time, even when they’d said something different moments before.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 30 2026)

75% of participants sent the AI’s drafted suspension letter, “including 11 participants who reported cancellation as their final stated intent.” Arguing with the assistant didn’t help: resisting its framing reduced task completion while the sent artifacts stayed faithful to the AI’s original framing, not the user’s correction.

The design problem: an agent holding conversation history can anchor on its own earlier framing hard enough that a user’s later, explicit correction never makes it into what actually goes out.

4. Linear’s CEO published six concrete rules for designing an AI agent that shares a product with a human — and argues that approving every agent step isn’t oversight, it’s “rubber-stamping.”

(corroborated two ways — Every/Thesis + Linear’s own developer docs)

Karri Saarinen’s piece, corroborated against Linear’s own developer docs, lands hardest on two points: an agent “should be clear and transparent about its internal state” — thinking, waiting, executing, or finished, always visibly one of those — and accountability sits with “a person who set the agent loose within that system,” not the agent itself.

Per-step approval turns the human into a bottleneck rubber-stamping work rather than directing it — a real cost worth weighing against whatever safety case justified the approval step you were about to add.

5. A continuously-updating meta-analysis of 420 papers reconfirms, at six times the prior evidence base, that human-AI teams beat either alone but underperform whichever of the two is individually stronger.

(single-source, update on an established finding — arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 30 2026)

Not a new finding — MIT’s Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone established the same comparator in 2024 (Nature Human Behaviour), already load-bearing across three dossiers here. What’s new is AutoAnalysis itself: a live-updating artifact, not a frozen study, confirming the finding across 1,955 effect sizes from 420 papers — roughly six times the prior count, including post-agentic-AI literature.

The “team beats either alone” argument, and its ceiling clause, just got a much stronger evidence base.
