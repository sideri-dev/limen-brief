AI refusals scold more than they repair — and that's a copywriting problem, not just a safety one.

The first pragmatics-based taxonomy of how language models decline requests looked at 16 models across 14 harm categories and found their refusals are explicit, morally evaluative judgments of the request itself — not the polite, face-saving deflections human conversation usually uses to soften a "no." When these systems do try to repair the interaction, they almost always do it by offering a safer alternative, rarely through ordinary interpersonal facework.

For anyone writing decline or refusal copy, that argues for building the alternative-offering move in by design and pulling back the moralizing tone, independent of whether the underlying refusal was actually warranted.

A uniform "AI confidence" badge can make a whole crowd wrong together.

A modeling paper extends the classic Bayesian information-cascade framework with an AI credibility score as a shared public signal, calibrated against real human-subject news-veracity data. Heavier reliance on that shared score keeps a crowd's correct beliefs stable, but it can just as easily lock in a wrong belief by crowding out the private, corrective impressions that would otherwise fix it — people weight the AI score above several peers' judgments, just below their own.

The paper's own remedy is structural: diversifying the AI signal shown to different users, rather than broadcasting one identical score to everyone, is what keeps a group's independent judgment alive.

Ambient AI in a meeting should whisper, not open a panel.

A system presented at UIST '26 listens to live discourse, identifies emerging information needs, and proactively retrieves relevant material — but surfaces it as small, ephemeral, self-dismissing "toast" notifications in a side channel, never a persistent chat window. In a 16-person comparative study, people using it reached better-informed decisions while the conversation kept its natural flow.

That's a genuine answer to the recurring ambient-design question of when an AI system should show itself: peripheral and transient beats modal and persistent for retrieval that happens mid-conversation.

By six tool-calls deep, an agent is running at roughly 30% of its own single-step reliability.

A new benchmark measures correct tool-invocation rate across contamination-free multi-step tasks, depths 1 through 8, on five open-weight models. By depth six, the model has lost about 70% of the clean-context capability it demonstrated at depth one — not to new problems, but to its own earlier mistakes compounding forward. The paper also shows the common exact-match scoring approach quietly bakes in assumptions about how severe or recoverable an error is, rather than actually measuring either.

For anyone deciding where to put a human checkpoint in an agentic chain, this gives an actual number to design against: treat depth six-plus as the point where autonomy should be interrupted, not the point where failure is eventually noticed.

Vercel's plain-English brand-style prompt for coding agents broke the moment two different models read it.

The team's first attempt at scaling brand consistency was a natural-language description of their internal design system, ported into a prompt for AI coding agents — and every model interpreted the same wording differently, generating visibly different pages from identical instructions. They replaced it with design.md, a versioned rules file paired with a blind A/B evaluation harness that ran well over 200 comparisons across Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5-based Codex.

On the final held-out test, pages built with design.md logged 39 failures against 91 for pages built without it. That kills "just write better instructions" as a durable strategy once more than one model is in the loop, and hands over a working template — a measured, model-agnostic artifact plus a repeatable eval harness — for anyone building design consistency into a multi-model agent pipeline.

Yesterday's dossier

The gaze-allocation HRI paper this corpus led with turns out to be a 5-page late-breaking report drawing on one 2023 dataset of 33 men that has now produced four papers, not the 18-page paper the digest recorded. Its two headline "eyes vs. mouth" effects are computed against all gaze samples in a role rather than against face-only gaze, and renormalizing the paper's own numbers collapses the eye effect to near zero while the mouth effect survives at about two-thirds its raw size. The face-level asymmetry the digest led with (people watching more while listening than speaking) is nearly identical to a published human-human result on the same eye-tracker that the paper never cites — it's a finding about conversation, not about robots. Where the robot genuinely differs is the mouth, getting roughly a third to a half of the attention a human mouth gets, which points at intelligibility rather than naturalness. Every shipped audio-driven face pipeline already spends its animation budget exactly where the paper recommends, so the paper's actionable half is a gap the stack already fills, not a gap to build toward.
