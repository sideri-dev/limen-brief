Where people actually look on a robot's face — and it flips depending on whether they're speaking or listening.

A secondary analysis of eye-tracking data from 31 people talking with a Furhat robot found gaze concentrates almost entirely on the eyes and mouth, but the split reverses by conversational role: people watch the face 95.8% of the time while listening versus 69.2% while speaking, and mouth-gaze roughly triples from 5.9% to 16.8% across that same shift (both large, well-powered effects). A separate exploratory analysis found that whether the robot itself averted or held its gaze changed where on the face people looked, but not whether they looked at the face at all.

That gives embodied-agent designers a real render-budget baseline — eyes and mouth first — and reframes a human's gaze aversion while speaking as normal behavior, not a disengagement signal to penalize.

The same explanation format helps trust calibration for one task type and actively hurts it for another.

A 204-person study tested graph visualizations of an LLM's reasoning against plain text explanations, split across verbal-reasoning tasks and visual-reasoning tasks. For verbal reasoning, the graph version improved how well people's trust tracked actual correctness — but felt more demanding and less satisfying to use. For visual reasoning it did the opposite: it impaired calibration while feeling more engaging and helpful.

The paper's own framing is the useful part — the format people prefer is not the format that produces better decisions, so a single explanation-UI default across output types is the wrong instinct.

A great demo output proves a system can do something, not that it will do it reliably.

Nielsen Norman Group's Raluca Budiu makes the case plainly: "one good output demonstrates that a system can perform a task. It does not show how often or how reliably the system will do so." Her worked example runs 10 questions across 5 repeated runs each, rather than trusting one polished pass — the same logic behind coding's pass@k benchmarks and Chatbot Arena's published confidence intervals.

The practical consequence: what you show a client should be a distribution across repeated runs, not a screenshot of your best output.

Handing a case to a human reviewer isn't a neutral safety valve — the mix of cases you defer changes how well that human performs.

A 226-person study found that when an AI's rejection queue skews heavily toward one class, the human reviewers who receive it get measurably worse at classifying the majority class correctly — regardless of which class was actually overrepresented. The authors call it a "test-taker's effect": an unbalanced stream of deferred cases distorts the reviewer's calibration, not just their workload.

That means a human-review queue needs to be designed for composition, not just volume — dumping whatever the model rejects into one undifferentiated queue can quietly degrade the humans handling it.

Figma scaled its qualitative user research to nearly 640 interviews by having AI moderate them — and the design decision that made the results trustworthy wasn't AI at all.

Figma's own writeup of its AI-impact index describes 639 transcripts, 2 million words, and over 40,000 codes distilled into 553 recurring themes — a scale no human research team runs. The team's stated principle: "we couldn't just punt the process to AI; we needed to design a system that enforced rigor at every step," meaning the coding method, granularity, and conflict-handling rules were fixed up front rather than left to the model.

The resulting index reportedly nearly doubled, from roughly 32 to 62 points (modeled on the University of Michigan's consumer sentiment index) since 2024. It's a concrete pattern for anyone scaling qualitative research with AI without losing methodological control — the rigor lives in the coding parameters, not the interview count.

Yesterday's dossier

The concept-bottleneck-model paper this corpus flagged as a dossier target turned out to be weaker than its own abstract: of the three conditions it claims gate when concept-level explanations help, one was never measured at all and another is inter-annotator agreement between two of the paper's own authors, never actually asked of participants. Read as an experiment rather than an abstract, every AI-supported condition across both studies is statistically indistinguishable from a plain label — the entire headline rests on one Bonferroni-corrected contrast at p=.049 after dropping over a third of one group. The trust claim collapses too: the eight-item trust index was never significant, and the one item that was says interaction lowered trust, the opposite of the paper's framing. An independent prior study (12 dermatology experts, 104 lay participants) had already found the same null. The defensible practitioner answer is mostly skip: build an interactive concept surface only where the concepts are objectively verifiable, few in number, and the user genuinely can't answer without them.
