1. Google deployed a persistent, proactive “AI teammate” across more than 20 teams — and the thing that broke wasn’t task performance, it was workplace norms.

Team Agent lives inside chat, docs and the bug tracker as a standing presence, not something invoked per task. A qualitative study — 17 interviews across 11 of the 20+ teams that adopted it — finds the friction sits in three places: unwritten collaborative norms the agent doesn’t know it’s breaking, what “relational boundaries” even mean for a non-human teammate, and a redistribution of trust and agency nobody designed for.

The lesson for practitioners: a proactive agent’s intervention rights can’t be scoped once at rollout — they get negotiated continuously, in situ, the way a new hire’s standing in a team gets negotiated. That’s the opposite problem from a one-time consent screen.

2. One ChatGPT interface, four countries, three years of data — “how people use AI” isn’t one answer, and it’s a moving target that moves differently by market.

A study of 202,590 conversations from 1,252 users in India, Nigeria, Brazil and Pakistan (Dec 2022–Feb 2026) finds self-expression use grew from a few percent of traffic to a fifth or more, while information-seeking only modestly declined and task delegation never grew at all. The country detail matters more than the aggregate: health topics dominate in India and Brazil, Urdu-English translation in Pakistan, current-affairs and religious inquiry in Nigeria and Pakistan.

For anyone designing one platform surface across Japanese, Thai and English markets, this argues directly against a single onboarding or affordance design traveling unchanged across locales.

3. Claude Sonnet 5.5 quietly changed what a streaming agent UI shows between tool calls — most will go silent unless someone catches it first.

Anthropic’s own docs state it plainly: text that used to stream between tool calls now defaults into thinking blocks, so “an application that streams that text to its users goes quiet between tool calls” unless it explicitly sets a display value or turns off up-front thinking. This is a concrete pre-upgrade audit item, not a benchmark change.

Any product that used visible intermediate reasoning as a liveness signal during tool use needs to check this before flipping the model. Ships with 1M context, 128K max output, $2/$10 per million tokens.

4. Making a companion robot feel eerier also made it more likeable — the “safe” choice, abstract vibration, is the empirically weaker one.

A within-subjects study (n=42) on the PARO robot compared no haptics, abstract vibration, and bio-physiological cues — heartbeat, purring, warmth. Bio-mimetic cues significantly increased perceived anthropomorphism, social warmth and likeability over both other conditions, even though they also measurably increased eeriness.

The authors call it the first empirical evidence that lifelike tactile cues enhance perceptions of a social robot — reframing the haptic-channel decision for any touch-surfaced embodied agent: the uncanny bump doesn’t need designing away if it’s buying warmth and likeability.

5. A scheduling fix, not new hardware, cuts full-duplex voice’s timing failures roughly 7x while running faster and using less memory.

DuplexCadence targets the exact failure mode that makes “listen while speaking” voice interfaces feel broken — speaking past the natural turn-taking window. Across four released full-duplex models and three decoder architectures, it drops the share of SPEAK turns overrunning the one-second cadence budget from 14% to 2%, while running at 2.85x stock-runtime speed, using 38.8% less peak memory, and producing bit-for-bit identical output.

Preprint, unreplicated — but if it holds, it’s the difference between budgeting new compute for natural interrupt-capable voice and just fixing the scheduler on what already ships.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s #1 item led with a year-long CHI Best Paper study naming “intuition rust” — AI quietly dulling expert judgment in oncology. Today’s dossier found the paper is qualitative, not the measured trajectory the digest implied: the n=42 is 34 clinicians plus 8 administrators, not “oncologists,” and the only quantitative figure in it is an unsourced “~15% faster.” The surrounding measurement literature doesn’t corroborate the claimed trajectory — it shows the trajectory hasn’t been measured: the one positive retrospective result is matched by a true withdrawal study of 13 endoscopists across 5,013 colonoscopies finding no upskilling or deskilling, the largest dataset in existence (~334,000 colonoscopies) calling the question “undetermined,” and a software-outage natural experiment running the opposite direction. What is robustly measured sits one layer down — in-the-moment anchoring on AI suggestions — and the paper’s real contribution is a set of instrumentable behavioral residues and a disclosure constraint on the telemetry that would detect them, not the eroding-skill trajectory it was read for.
