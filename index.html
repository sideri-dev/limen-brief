1. Anthropic’s own usage data says per-action approval stops working as agent users gain experience — continuous monitoring plus interrupt is what replaces it.

Anthropic’s telemetry across real Claude Code usage finds 73% of tool calls already have some human in the loop, yet only 0.8% of all actions are irreversible — so most of what gets approved didn’t strictly need gating. The sharper finding is behavioral: as people get more experienced with an agent, they don’t approve every action more carefully, they auto-approve more while also intervening more — shifting from gatekeeping to supervision.

That’s a direct argument against the default “insert an approval modal before every action” pattern baked into most agentic UIs; the oversight mechanism that survives contact with real use is visibility plus the ability to interrupt, not a checkpoint on every step.

2. GitHub rebuilt its pull-request review renderer because AI-scale PRs broke the obvious approach — a per-block ResizeObserver that can retrigger itself under its own output.

Stress-tested against a 2,200-file, million-plus-line, 400-comment PR, GitHub’s team designed and then rejected the straightforward fix — one ResizeObserver watching each collapsible block — because an observer that writes height back into layout can retrigger itself, and that cost compounds with every mounted block. The shipped replacement splits rendering into two virtualized domains instead.

It’s a concrete, named architectural decision, not a feature post, for any review surface that now has to survive pull requests an order of magnitude larger than anything a human alone would generate.

3. A service robot that lies and gets lucky doesn’t get forgiven the way a human would — people hold it to an outcome-independent honesty standard, at least in adversarial complaint contexts.

(single-source, not peer-reviewed)

A Japanese HAI Symposium study ran two between-subjects scenarios (N=150 and N=156) where a dishonest statement either turned out true or false. For a human who told the same lie, blame dropped sharply once the lie came true; for a robot making the identical statement, blame stayed flat and ran significantly higher than the human’s, specifically in the adversarial complaint scenario.

Don’t design a complaint-handling or customer-facing agent assuming a lie that “worked out” will be forgiven the way it would for a person — keep the agent’s statements honest regardless of outcome, especially wherever the interaction is already adversarial.

4. A companion robot’s over-proximity reads as intrusive no matter how close the relationship is supposed to be — relationship depth moves the minimum comfortable distance, not the ceiling for what counts as too close.

(single-source, not peer-reviewed — fuller version of a bare mention from September 27)

A Kansai University HAI Symposium study varied a robot’s physical distance, dwell time, and stated relationship (stranger, acquaintance, close friend) across 24 analyzed participants. Close approach and long dwell raised discomfort and annoyance regardless of relationship depth, rejecting the authors’ own hypotheses that familiarity would buy tolerance for either — and a “close friend” agent that stayed short and distant was rated as cold and aloof instead.

For any embodied agent carrying relationship state, this means “we have history” licenses a lower floor of engagement, not permission to escalate proximity or dwell time as a stand-in for warmth. The paper surfaced once before with no numbers attached; this is the first time the actual statistics reached a reader.

5. Microsoft’s Fluent 2 design system specifies exact required components for responsible-AI patterns, not aspirational principles.

The guidance names concrete, implementable requirements: an error-recovery pattern that must “name what failed, explain why, and offer two paths forward,” an agent-memory pattern requiring users understand what gets saved and how it’s used, and an autonomy-disclosure pattern requiring an agent to communicate its own triggers, access permissions and action rights.

That specificity is what separates this from most “responsible AI” guidance — it’s a checklist a team can build against and audit, not a values statement. The page itself carries no version or date stamp, worth knowing before citing it later.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s dossier substantially extends, not corrects, the digest’s #1 item from October 4 — the “omnirelevance” voice-agent paper. It confirms the core finding (people manage their speech around the risk an agent might wrongly act on it) but shows the burden has already changed shape: as capture quality improved, people shifted from leaning in to be heard to whispering and gesturing to stay unheard, and the paper’s own closing question was already answered in 2019 by research it cites.

Every adjacent literature checked — developer APIs, acoustic addressee-detection research, the Japanese 相槌 backchannel tradition — confirms the burden is real and that nobody has built a remedy for it beyond headphones and a quieter room. The paper’s load-bearing premise (that today’s agents rely on simple silence-based turn-taking) is already superseded for the single largest deployed system, which went full-duplex 26 days after the paper published. The design fix is not new invention — a 2019 gaze-gated system already demonstrated it — the gap is adoption, not research.
