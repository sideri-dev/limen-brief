1. Multi-agent AI deliberation gets its accuracy gain from going back and forth, not from adding more agents — and a built-in devil’s advocate backfires.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, well-powered)

Two user studies (N=401, N=417) isolating what drives multi-agent deliberation’s benefit found it’s multiple rounds of exchange, not agent headcount, that produced the accuracy gain over single-round AI assistance. A built-in “devil’s advocate” persona, added on the assumption friction sharpens judgment, instead hurt both accuracy and user experience.

Active human participation raised accuracy on one task, at a real cost in frustration. For multi-agent review surfaces: spend effort on rounds, not headcount, and test an adversarial persona rather than assume it helps.

2. People trust a consistently wrong AI almost as much as a consistently right one — until they’re shown it’s wrong.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Four experiments found people learn to prefer a helpful AI over an unhelpful one only once given direct accuracy feedback (67% vs. 21%). Without that feedback, a reliably wrong model still persuades people 48% of the time, barely behind a genuinely helpful one’s 38% — consistent tone alone buys a bad advisor almost as much trust as being right.

People also trusted AI advisors over human ones giving the same advice (83% vs. 65%). The lever: a trust surface must expose outcome feedback, not just persona consistency.

3. Winning a user’s ear and winning the authority to act for them are two different design problems.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A 121-person study found the advisor people most wanted to talk to (40%) reasoned through the user’s own values but still recommended the opposite decision — reasoning alignment beat agreement. As the advisor’s role shifted toward acting on the user’s behalf, preference flipped toward decision-matching advisors, and opt-out nearly tripled, from 12% consulting to 38% delegating.

A good advice-giver is not automatically ready to be handed delegated authority — different trust relationships, different requirements.

4. Voice-agent evaluation needs two listening tests, because “sounds human” and “I’d rather talk to this” are different judgments — and naturalness scores predict neither.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Two listening studies on 120 customer-service exchanges found listeners chose an AI system over a human actor in 19% of human-likeness trials but 36% of conversational-preference trials (49% for the best system). None of three naturalness scores predicted conversational preference; two predicted lower human-likeness.

If the design goal is sustained engagement, not passing as human, naturalness is the wrong instrument.

5. Live transcription systems lose whispered speech badly, and can silently drop the non-English half of a bilingual conversation.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, methodology benchmark)

A benchmark of 11 live streaming ASR systems found Zoom Scribe and OpenAI’s GPT-Live-Transcribe nearly tied on quiet English (7.4% vs. 7.3% WER) but diverging sharply whispered (34.7% vs. 8.8%); pairing Whisper with a standard voice-activity detector pushed whispered WER from 10.1% to 51.4%.

English-locked, Gemini, Deepgram and self-hosted Whisper all lost the non-English half of a two-language recording 86-99% of the time — a vanishing turn, not degraded text. For bilingual dictation: test the VAD-and-model pairing together, never ship single-language-locked transcription where speakers code-switch.

Yesterday’s dossier re-read the paper behind the previous dossier target — an offline first-aid assistant running a 4-bit Gemma model on a Pixel 7 Pro — and found its two headlines belong to different systems: the 6.86s/14.88s phone timings measured the fine-tuned model alone, while every accuracy score, including the “best” 2.51/5, ran on a GPU config never tested on the phone.

Re-auditing the training corpus found it doesn’t just lack first-aid detail, it sometimes teaches wrong answers outright, while two “missing” details the paper flagged aren’t actually in the guideline it checked against. No sub-4B on-device model has yet produced complete, guideline-adherent free text in this domain, and the latency that matters — 27-34s to a first chest compression — makes 6.86s a fraction of the real wait.

This week

Three patterns converged. What an agent’s context keeps or discards is a behavioral control surface, not a storage question — compacting memory, scrubbing a failed tool call, and letting benign notes persist each changed behavior in ways no reading of the text would predict; never let memory function as authorization, since text can’t predict what the model does with it.

A task’s measured outcome and its self-reported trust score moved independently, in both directions, across five studies this week — one redesign improved correctness while trust stayed flat, another raised cognitive load while trust rose — so task outcome, not trust score, should decide whether a design works.

And two field-strength results agreed ambient-agent presence should be scheduled to task events, not accumulated trust: a retail agent timing its own appearance/disappearance beat an always-present baseline on real purchase conversion, while the popular “taper as trust builds” disclosure schedule was found never tested — its trigger is the best-replicated antecedent of automation complacency in the human-factors literature.
