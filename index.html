Today

1. A controlled study finds that understanding how an AI writing assistant works can make you worse at catching its mistakes.

An N=48 CHI 2026 lab study gave participants different mental models of an AI writing assistant. The group given the deeper “structural” model understood the system better and rated it more usable — but produced letters with more grammatical errors slipping through, because that same deeper understanding correlated with less scrutiny of its flawed suggestions. The authors call it a “complex relationship between system understanding, trust, and control in contexts that require user oversight.”

The read for practice: explaining “how it works” is not an error-catching mechanism. If a product needs real oversight, it needs an actual verification affordance, not a better explanation.

2. Withdrawal studies on an AI advisor find the help doesn’t fully evaporate — but only on one measure, and how often you pushed back against it predicts how well you do alone.

A preprint ran two preregistered experiments (N=200, n=198) in a simulated factory task, with and without an LLM advisor, then measured unaided performance after withdrawal. Previously-supported participants beat controls when scored against repeating their earlier choices — but not against the task’s defaults — and the strongest predictor of good unaided performance was how often someone had overridden the AI while it was available.

Evaluating an advisor on with-assistance performance alone hides whether anything durable was learned; override frequency is a cheap leading indicator of what sticks.

3. A field deployment of a home reminder robot finds families want it to interrupt them unasked, and that failing silently is worse than failing loudly.

Researchers co-designed a reminder system with 10 families, then deployed it in 8 homes for four days with a modified Temi robot. Of 77 delivered reminders, 49 were triggered proactively by the robot noticing something via vision; the rest were users checking in on a screen. Families preferred proactive delivery — one parent said it “took some of the emotion out of it.” The sharper finding is about failure: the robot’s navigation often failed silently, and families didn’t realize anything was wrong until much later, which frustrated them more than an obvious failure would have.

Two decisions for in-home agents: default to proactive, sensor-triggered delivery over a passive check-in screen, and build explicit help-seeking behavior — a quiet failure is the worse one.

4. Superhuman killed “every integration is its own chatbot” and said why.

Superhuman’s Packs SDK blog documents walking back its original architecture, where every connected app surfaced as its own chattable agent. In their words: “we found that the approach didn’t scale to more complex use cases, where an agent needs to work across multiple applications and services.” The fix: integrations now surface as connectors — bundles of tools, data and skills — composed by a separate orchestrating agent, rather than forcing users between per-integration chat panels. Three old APIs are deprecated outright.

A concrete, stated-reasoning retreat from a popular multi-app agent pattern, six weeks old but durable as a design argument.

5. shadcn/ui shipped a standard way to script and test human-in-the-loop approval flows.

The changelog describes a helper that can “mock human-in-the-loop flows for the AI SDK” — a scripted conversation that pauses for real input, waits on an approval, and resumes with whatever the user decided, streaming through the same lifecycle production chat uses. It ships with a needsApproval parameter as a first-class, testable primitive.

Infrastructure, not a feature announcement: it standardizes the vocabulary for an approval/pause state at the design-system level, independent of which model sits behind the product.

Three patterns from this week’s synthesis:

The human-facing autonomy dial doesn’t really exist — the working control is a visible boundary on the tool’s scope. Across Cursor, Google’s Team Agent, a four-UI oversight study, Linear and Adobe, every attempt at an adjustable “how much autonomy” setting was inert, never built, or quietly moved to the machine side. What works is a typed, displayed, revertible scope — withheld wherever that scope can’t be shown, because the measured failure mode is a grant whose class the system computed but never displayed.

This week’s loudest “AI erodes expert skill” claims rest on quotes and null results, not measurement. The CHI Best Paper’s “intuition rust” is a participant’s phrase, not a trend; a widely-cited “productivity-experience paradox” reports a non-significant result (p=.195) as if established; a true withdrawal study of 13 endoscopists across 5,013 procedures found no measurable change at all. The defensible client-room deliverable is a seeded-probe or behavioral-residue instrument, not the erosion story itself.

The same chat interface produces different use patterns by country, and the dominant “AI as oracle” framing is itself US-centric. A four-country ChatGPT study found self-expression use rising while delegation stayed flat, split by topic per country; a Japanese government survey found a reference-tool-authority model displacing the US’s friend/counselor framing entirely. No Thai sample exists anywhere in the corpus yet — named here, not papered over.
