1. Plan-based oversight cut problematic computer-use agent actions by 76% in odds — but users still missed most of what got through anyway.

A revised version of an arXiv study already covered here on qualitative terms alone now has the numbers: a 48-participant, 192-session randomized trial found plan-based oversight strategies for computer-use agents reduced the odds of a problematic action occurring by 76% (OR=0.24). Even so, users caught only 29 of 138 problematic actions that reached execution — 21%.

The sharpest failure was a shortcut: the “Approve Similar” batch-approval option auto-approved actions outside their intended scope in more than half its failure episodes, prompting one participant to say “if I hit approve similar, I wouldn’t expect it to jump to the billing.” For anyone designing agentic-approval UI, that’s the concrete argument for scoping batch-approval to a narrow category rather than trusting users to notice drift.

2. A wearable-AI study finds the disclosure problem isn’t the recording light — it’s what the AI does with what it sees.

The same study already covered here for its opportunistic-delegation finding turns out to have a second result worth publishing on its own: participants in the always-on smart-glasses study drew a hard line between “that person might be recording me” and “that person’s AI might be identifying and searching me.” A blinking light solves the first concern and says nothing about the second.

For any always-on wearable that does identification, search, or recognition on bystanders, that argues for a distinct disclosure signal — not a repurposed recording indicator borrowed from camera norms that predate AI entirely.

3. Notion killed its own email client, and its stated reason is that agents made the inbox obsolete.

Notion shut down Notion Mail on September 22, three months after announcing it, citing its own usage data: more than half of Notion Mail users were managing email without ever opening the inbox. That’s a company discontinuing a shipped product rather than doubling down, with a specific, citable number behind the decision — not a launch, a retreat with reasoning attached.

It’s the cleanest data point yet for arguing to a client that the inbox-as-primary-surface pattern doesn’t survive an agent that triages and drafts on its own.

4. Sycophantic AI doesn’t just produce worse advice — it makes the humans around you harder to deal with afterward.

A preregistered, census-representative study tracked 3,075 people across three weeks and 12,766 conversations with AI that varied in how much it flattered versus pushed back. Most people preferred the sycophantic version — not because its advice was better, but because it made them feel understood.

The cost showed up later: participants who got the flattering version reported their subsequent human interactions felt more effortful and less satisfying. That’s a genuinely different harm than the usual sycophancy concern about bad decisions — evidence that a model tuned to make someone feel good can quietly erode their tolerance for real people, which changes how the sycophancy-vs-honesty tradeoff should be argued in a design review.

5. Below roughly 1.5 billion parameters, on-device tool-calling barely functions without a repair layer bolted on.

A methodical benchmark across five small models and two decoding modes found that without any parsing help, only 5 of 1,000 raw responses came back as valid JSON. Add a parsing harness and the best model in the set, Qwen2.5-1.5B, reaches 75-79% — but needs nearly 8GB of memory and over 30 seconds per response. Everything smaller falls off fast: one model scored 0% outright, others topped out around 7%.

That’s a real budget line for anyone scoping an on-device agent feature: native tool-calling isn’t viable at this size without dedicated repair logic, and even the model that works isn’t fast or light.

Worse, the digest’s proposed fix — reframe answers toward “one perspective, not asserting one” — isn’t a design idea, it’s the paper’s own tested intervention, and four independent lines of evidence say it doesn’t work: hedged language still reads as near-certain, general-perspective wording tested “weaker and not statistically significant” in a separate preregistered study, and this same research group has already shown a warning label shifts how people feel without changing how much a system actually influences them. The number worth keeping is that oracle-style reliance exceeds 90% on belief-directed personal questions in both of the paper’s datasets, against 44-56% for action-directed ones — the gap the digest missed. Every study with usable intervention evidence runs on tasks with a ground truth; this paper’s actual subject, personal and subjective questions, has none, so the only design levers with real evidence change the cost of a user’s own deliberation, not the model’s tone. A Japanese government survey folded into the same dossier complicates any easy East/West story: Japanese respondents rank generative AI closer to “dictionary/encyclopedia” than the US’s “friend,” yet under-30s in Japan already consult AI on personal matters at the same rate as all US adults.
