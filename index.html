Nobody shipping a smart-home app in China has built a consent screen for the people who live there but don't own the account.

A CHI 2026 audit of 49 apps found 19 recognize that bystanders — housemates, guests, cleaners — can be harmed by the app's sensing, but none offer them consent or notification for registration, permissions, or data deletion. 36 of the 49 apps can operate cameras, 27 can control door locks.

The gap is a missing screen, not a missing policy: shared-space sensing products need a first-class non-account-holder consent surface, distinct from the primary user's settings. It converges with the independent Korean "OurData" study published here five weeks ago — same structural gap, different country and method.

When a chatbot is wrong, have it correct itself out loud — routing the fix through a separate fact-check layer costs trust.

A controlled experiment (N=120) found three correction methods — webpage retraction, self-correction, separate-chatbot correction — fixed the error equally well, but only self-correction avoided damaging trust. Self-correcting chatbots rated significantly higher on both trustworthiness and perceived expertise.

Users' social connection to a chatbot only converted into belief change when the same chatbot corrected itself; outsourcing the correction to a separate source severed that link entirely. Single-source finding — no independent replication yet.

arXiv 2606.19286 →

What a driving alert says, not just whether it fires, is what earns trust — and the effect flips sharply by age.

A 240-person driving-simulator study found plain object notifications ("there is a car in front of you on the right") beat vaguer or alarm-framed phrasing on both acceptance and adaptive-cruise-control comfort, with a significant Condition × Age interaction (p<0.001): older drivers responded more to alert-framed language, younger drivers to plain description.

There's no single best default phrasing for an ADAS notification — specificity, tuned by driver age, is the supported design lever. Single-source, peer-reviewed.

OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra pauses for human review only on its own chat surfaces — the API just stops.

Astra crosses OpenAI's "Critical" cybersecurity threshold under its Preparedness Framework, scoring a perfect 100% on ExploitBench against 78.5% for GPT-5.6 Sol. The confirm-dialog safety behavior is scoped to ChatGPT and Codex only — the model card states that in the API, a paused task simply stops, with no review prompt and no path to continue.

Anyone building on the API rather than the chat surface needs a different failure-mode plan: a hard stop with no recovery, not an interruptible confirm dialog.

Google swapped Gemini's numeric "thinking budget" for three named tiers — low, medium, high.

Gemini 3.8 Flash replaces the thinking_budget token count with a thinking_level enum: low for latency-critical tasks like incident response or real-time chat, medium (the default) for complex code and agentic use, high for deep multi-step reasoning — each mapped by Google to explicit task types.

The design shift is real: picking a discrete, named tier rather than calibrating a number. Introductory pricing ($0.75/1M input, $3.75/1M output) runs only "through the end of year," per the page's own wording, not a fixed date.

Yesterday's dossier

A re-read of yesterday's #1 item (the five-session trust-dip study) found the digest reported the wrong finding. Trust medians are identical across all five sessions, "recovery" was never tested, and evaluators' actual rate of ignoring the assistant's suggestions gets worse after session 2 and stays worse through session 4.

The paper's own better explanation isn't a fading novelty effect but calibration: evaluators couldn't tell a 93%-precision assistant from an 80%-precision one until a second exposure — a durable correction, not decay. Two reporting anomalies (identical statistics across unrelated measures) raise integrity questions the dossier flags but can't resolve. The timing lesson survives even though "dip and recovery" doesn't: a single-session pilot will misjudge assistant-quality perception.

This week

Eval numbers are conditional, and the condition is usually the tooling's default, not a choice anyone made. Five sources this week varied one axis of an eval's condition — repetition, accessibility display settings, model identity, agent chain depth, session count — and each flipped a ship/no-ship call. Sharpest case: no major computer-use benchmark varies display scale at all. A number without its condition envelope isn't a finding.

Four jurisdictions are legislating a uniform AI-content label this quarter; the week's evidence says uniform labels are the failure mode. The EU AI Act's Article 50 is now in force with no conformity-presumption route; China, India and Thailand still haven't specified what the mark should look like. A calibrated Bayesian model shows a shared, identical credibility signal can lock a crowd into wrong beliefs — diversifying it per user is the fix, and no statute forbids that.

The "it's only an AI" discount doesn't exist, measured three ways. A Japanese replication (N=201) found blame for lying statistically identical whether the liar is human or robot. A 16-model refusal taxonomy found declines read as moral conduct, hedging switched off entirely. An airport robot study found flawless navigation still reads as a violation when it misreads the room. What actually recovers trust is an offered alternative or partial compliance — not an apology.
