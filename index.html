1. A pre-registered, 9,341-person, six-country study finds that disclosure captions on AI demonstration videos work only as long as they stay on screen.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Without any disclosure, viewers already call real-vs-CGI footage correctly 62% of the time and autonomous-vs-teleoperated behavior correctly 52% of the time — better than chance, nowhere near confident. Adding a disclosure caption measurably sharpens both judgments, but when the same survey later showed viewers undisclosed clips, the improvement vanished — the paper’s own words are that the effect was “no longer distinguishable from zero.”

For anyone building or reviewing a capability demo reel, this kills the idea that one disclosure at the top does lasting work: calibration needs the label on every clip, not just the first. Research: Nozomi Nakajima, arXiv:2611.18982 (cs.RO).

2. Japan’s actors’ union has published a staged, written-consent framework for AI voice use, and it draws a hard line at death.

(two independent sources)

日本俳優連合’s new guideline (a preamble plus 22 articles) requires a performer’s written consent at each of three separate stages — training, generation, and project use of the generated audio — capped at one year and renewable, with retroactive-damages language clawing back revenue from unauthorized use. Separately, per Kyodo/Kumanichi’s reporting specifically (not the corroborating bengo4.com account, which doesn’t carry this claim), the guideline asks that a deceased performer’s voice stay out of new work and that AI not substitute for dubbing, with an exception only where family or estate consent is given.

It’s a concrete, usable template — staged consent, a time cap, a posthumous carve-out — for anyone building voice-cloning or dubbing into the Japanese market; 34 days old, but self-regulatory documents don’t age out like capability claims. Research: reported by 弁護士ドットコムニュース (塚田賢慎) and 共同通信/熊本日日新聞.

3. Google is retiring Gems for an open, portable “Skills” format, and the reasoning is the real story.

(vendor-primary; corroborated independently)

Skills apply tailored instructions directly inside a conversation or workflow instead of forcing open a separate Gem window, built on an open Markdown spec (SKILL.md) explicitly pitched against platform lock-in. Rollout is dated precisely: Gemini app October 13, Workspace November 9, Gems moved into Settings yesterday, Business/Enterprise keeps old-style Gems until “no sooner than” March 1, 2027.

The lesson travels past Google: a locked, single-window custom-instruction pattern is a dead end — the fix is modular, composable instructions, not a dedicated chat window per customization. Research: Google Workspace Help.

4. A new control scheme for proactive AI agents splits “is it watching” from “is it acting” — and logging what it quietly chose not to do turns out to matter a lot.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

SpotMe, flagged independently by two of this corpus’s research threads this week, lets someone turn down an agent’s participation — to just asking, or to outright silence — without also having to turn off its sensing. The sharper finding comes from a civic-dashboard follow-up: showing people the actions an agent silently decided to skip rated as genuinely clarifying (average 6.44 out of 7), and one participant described a visibly-withheld action as reading like “a calculated decision” rather than an absence.

For anyone designing ambient or sensor-driven agents, the lesson is to decouple the sensing switch from the action switch, and keep a visible record of what the agent chose not to do, not just what it did. Research: Khan, Ali and Huang, arXiv:2611.22431.

5. More reasoning effort makes a shopping agent sound more sure of itself without making it right more often.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Across ten frontier models and three shopping benchmarks (WebShop, Amazon SQID, Wayfair WANDS), turning up how hard a model “thinks” before answering produced no statistically meaningful gain in decision quality — in the researchers’ own words, it “buys confidence rather than correctness.”

That kills a design instinct showing up across agent UX: surfacing “the agent thought hard about this” as a trust signal. If effort doesn’t track accuracy, showing it off just dresses up overconfidence. Research: Krishnamoorthy and Han, arXiv:2611.22033.

Yesterday’s dossier

The MirrorBot item rested on a press release, but the HRI ’26 paper and its CHI ’26 companion are both openly readable and say less than the release did. In the 40-pair controlled study, strangers with the mirror robot talked for 46.3% of the session and strangers left alone in an empty room talked for 45.8% (p = 0.977) — the robot’s advantage is in self-reported ease of starting a conversation, not in measured behavior, and no eye-contact variable exists in either study.

“Knowing when to step back” was a rule the designers wrote before the study and executed by a human operator on a roughly five-minute exit schedule, not autonomous judgment. The underlying mechanism — a shared object that gives strangers an excuse to connect — was named “triangulation” by William H. Whyte in 1980 and cited by neither paper. The real cost of over-persistence is better measured outside HRI entirely: clinical-reminder acceptance drops 30% per added reminder in a single encounter.
