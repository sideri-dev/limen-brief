1. A new paper names the exact fix for agents that fail when the software underneath them changes mid-task — and it isn’t reminding the agent to check.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, EnvOS, submitted Oct 29 2026)

EnvOS: a clinic-booking agent goes from 0/16 successes unaided, to 2/16 when told to re-verify the source, to 15/16 when the current value is simply shown again — a gain holding after correcting for multiple comparisons. An insurance-claims agent shows the same pattern, 1/10 to 9/10.

The paper’s grader is itself usable: it rejected all 26 planted-clean cases and caught 25 of 39 external failures, against 4 of 39 for a simple log check. For any agent on a backend that changes, the decision this settles: re-display current state, don’t just prompt verification.

2. English clears the same automated confidence gate 8.6 to 11.7 points more often than fifty other languages — even on equally correct answers.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 17 2026)

Three vendors’ production classifier APIs, tested on 1,000 items each in 51 languages at a fixed 0.9 threshold, preregistered. The gap survives the obvious fixes: translating into English nearly closes it, a per-language calibrated threshold does not.

A structural caution against treating confidence-gated automation — moderation, routing, auto-approval — as language-neutral by default, in any market outside English.

3. A real clinician interview study finds decision-support trust runs on case complexity and peer discussion, not the accuracy-badge variables most lab studies test.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, N=12 survey + N=10 interviews)

Twelve survey respondents and ten interviewees, two clinical cohorts already using a decision-support system, converge: reliance shifts with experience, predictions matter more in complex cases, decisions get discussed with colleagues, trust depends on workflow fit.

Small sample, a lead not a settled finding — but it points at workflow integration as the variable worth designing for over a bare accuracy display.

4. A 65-radiologist survey finds trust in an AI stroke-triage tool depends almost entirely on whether the AI agrees with the radiologist, not on how accurate it is.

(single-source — JMIR Human Factors 2026;13:e92145)

Only 18.5% found the false-positive rate acceptable, but the real finding is the asymmetry: 50.8% trust the AI when it agrees with their read, 3.1% when it conflicts.

Not a calibration problem better accuracy fixes — a structural feature of how a second opinion gets trusted, usable on any interface pairing an AI judgment with an expert’s own.

5. LogicTree swaps the chat window for a persistent, editable tree of document objects — and measures better logical quality, not just a preference bump.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, N=12 within-subjects)

A 12-person study on academic writing: the structured-object interface reduced workload and improved measured logical quality against a conversational baseline.

Small N, but a testable alternative to “just use chat” for AI co-writing, with an outcome measure behind it.

Full-text reading of yesterday’s #1 item (the 2001 Army Research Laboratory automation-bias report) narrows it sharply: its headline 93%-vs-77% figures are relayed secondhand from an unpublished manuscript, with no N, SD or test, and its one inferential statistic doesn’t reproduce from its own cells.

The manuscript’s published version (Human Factors 44(1), 2002) reports self-report favored automated aids while measured performance showed more disuse of them — a 2002 instance of the stated/measured dissociation this corpus already tracks from 2023 on. A same-lab replication found the gap only with a raw error-count elicitation; a 2023 preregistered study found the ordering inverts against a human expert. What survives: not a 16-point number but three conditions to check before trusting any stated-expectation figure — elicitation format, comparator expertise, self-report vs. actual reliance.
