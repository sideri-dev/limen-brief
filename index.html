1. An engineer at Linear treats scheduling an AI agent as delegation, not automation — and when a loop misbehaved, he had the agent rewrite its own instructions instead of hand-tuning them.

(single-source, vendor-primary — shipped feature, own design reasoning)

Linear’s new “Loops” let an engineer hand recurring work to the Linear Agent on a schedule or trigger. Andrew Moedinger: “I try to think about automation the way you might think about delegation,” escalating a cleanup loop from identify-only to create-batches to direct-assign over time, under the rule “start small, both in the scope of the task and in the consequences of getting it wrong.” When a meeting-prep loop grew too verbose and searched the wrong Slack channel, he didn’t fine-tune its prompt by hand — “I asked the Linear Agent to update the loop and run it again.” Loops stay visible at team level, so peer review substitutes for approval.

The transferable pattern: grant scheduled-agent authority on scope and consequence first, and treat letting the agent edit its own instructions as a legitimate alternative to manual prompting.

2. A five-year interview study of Chinese digital painters finds creative-AI delegation boundaries get renegotiated every year, not settled once.

(single-source, preprint — durable field method)

Seventeen painters, interviewed annually from 2021 to 2025, describe what the authors call “longitudinal agency partitioning” — the ongoing work of deciding which stages and claims stay human. By 2025 the group had split: some built stable hybrid workflows around human-only zones, others reported “fatigue, precarity, or difficulty locating a remaining human role.”

If boundaries move with peer norms and capability over years, a creative-AI feature’s human/AI split can’t be a fixed first-use setting — it needs to stay reopenable.

3. Trust and perceived competence in an AI response peak at moderate validation, then fall if the AI gets either colder or more sycophantic.

(single-source, preprint — mechanism study)

A Tsinghua study built a five-level validation framework and a sycophancy-perception scale, running it across 1,003 retained responses to personal and work conflicts. Trust and competence both varied sharply and non-monotonically with validation strength (F(4,9009)=61.3 and 52.0, both p<.001), peaking at moderate validation and dropping on both sides toward coldness or full sycophancy. Across ten models, Claude Sonnet 4.6 scored lowest on generated sycophancy; Gemini 3.1 Pro, GLM 5.1, and Doubao Seed 1.8 scored highest.

That gives tone design a measurable target instead of the usual vague “warm but not sycophantic.”

4. A misleading chart cuts web-agent task success by more than 34 points, and the finding is now peer-reviewed, not just a preprint.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — accepted EMNLP 2026)

MisVisAgentBench ran 280 matched clean/misleading chart pairs against thirteen vision-language web agents and found swapping in a misleading chart dropped average task success by more than 34 percentage points. It was logged as an unreviewed preprint; raw fetch found an EMNLP 2026 acceptance in the paper’s own metadata, moving it up the ranking under the standing rule that peer review outranks a bare benchmark.

The duty this names: any computer-use or browsing agent reading a chart as tool input needs an independent verification step — the reading itself can’t be assumed reliable.

5. LongHarness swaps an agent’s growing chat history for explicit evidence/conclusion state, and the gain is large on 100-million-token document sets.

(single-source, preprint)

The authors’ complaint: “acquired evidence and evolving conclusions often remain entangled in a growing interaction history,” which drifts as the history grows. Replacing it with a maintained evidence/conclusion state lifted one model’s exact-match score on a long-document benchmark from 15.8% to 60.2% on one set and 4.4% to 61.1% on another, beating Claude Code by 12 points and Codex by nearly 19.

The architecture choice this names: for any agent reasoning over more material than fits its context window, track evidence and conclusions as explicit state, not as chat history.
