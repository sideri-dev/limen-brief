1. A 625-person randomized trial finds taking a deliberate break from AI measurably reduces loneliness — and the effect outlasts the break itself.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Active ChatGPT users randomized to a 14-day deactivation reported significantly less loneliness than a 1-day-control group, an effect still present two weeks after they resumed using AI, alongside a real rise in in-person contact.

First directly causal test of a question this corpus had only approached correlationally — gives a field-RCT basis for a deliberate usage-break feature as a wellbeing choice, not an engagement-maximization one. Research: Zhao, Wang, Zhang, Ibrahim, Kraut & Yang, arXiv:2611.25263 (cs.HC/cs.CY).

2. HAI 2026’s Best Paper found AI assistance in a hybrid interface cuts effort without making anyone faster — and who delegates is a personality trait, not a task property.

(HAI 2026 Best Paper, Osaka)

Seventy-three people doing CRUD tasks across traditional, AI-first and hybrid modes saw significantly fewer clicks and less scrolling with AI assistance, but no faster completion; individual differences explained roughly half the variance in how much people delegated.

Argues for a delegation default set per user, not per task, and for treating “feels easier” and “got done faster” as separate metrics. Research: Dizon, Sta. Maria, Deja & Sumi, “Delegating or Doing?”, arXiv:2608.19551.

3. Using AI for work tasks measurably reduces how much you ask colleagues for help — unless the AI use is visible.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A 248-worker vignette experiment found AI-enabled work reduced stated help-seeking toward colleagues, especially for brainstorming, while visible (versus hidden) AI use pushed it back up; individual contributors looked more exposed than managers.

Stated-intention data, not observed behavior — but it names a cheap, concrete lever: a “drafted/assisted with AI” badge by default. Research: Chow, Deng, He, Min, Wang, Saha & Das Swain, arXiv:2611.25269.

4. Anthropic explains why it never built one all-purpose “security agent,” and why a new AI reviewer only gets authority after running silently in the background first.

(single-source — vendor’s own account)

Multiple narrowly-scoped reviewers catch each other’s blind spots in a way one broad reviewer can’t, Anthropic’s deputy CISO argues; the newest reviewer’s catch rate rose from 16% to 54% under this structure, now catching roughly a third of pre-ship bugs.

New reviewers run silently, risk-weighted, before anyone trusts their verdicts. Research: Anthropic, Jason Clinton (Deputy CISO).

5. Chrome’s built-in local AI model can be driven end-to-end with no user interaction at all, and its one-time permission prompt is not a real safeguard.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A new security paper finds Chrome’s one-time activation check before downloading Gemini Nano is “easily satisfied with a simulated click or bypassed entirely”; once resident, the model is invisible to network monitoring and vendor-signed.

Anyone building on window.ai needs their own consent gate — Chrome’s isn’t one. Research: Fleshman & Lucas, “Living off the Local Model,” arXiv:2611.25086 (cs.CR).

Yesterday’s dossier

A backfill dossier read five full-text experiments testing whether the user or the system should control how much support someone gets, and in none did the user’s half move a performance or learning measure — only self-reported effort moved, and in one study self-efficacy favored system control. The corpus’s one apparent counter-case doesn’t survive full text: its win for shared control is a bundle of four unrelated motivational features against none, and the de-confounded contrast is a flat null.

Five learner-control meta-analyses on record now all sit between −0.04 and +0.05, and none of this has ever been tested on an AI assistant. Takeaway: “let the user choose their support level” is performance-neutral in small novice studies, not a result you can cite with a number yet.

This week

Three patterns from this week’s synthesis. First: whether an AI agent behaves safely is governed by what a tool’s return actually shows it, not by prompts or policy text — a bare count left models acting on the wrong account 54% of the time where listing the competing record cut that to 20%, but every fix tried carried its own measured cost, up to a full completion collapse. Treat a tool’s return shape as a priced tradeoff, not a free fix.

Second: the controls users actually touch — sliders, effort toggles — are measured almost nowhere. Two published rulers for the same benchmark rank identical methods in opposite orders, and turning up a shopping agent’s reasoning effort buys felt confidence, not better decisions. Log realized adherence per setting from day one; stop treating “show how hard it thought” as an uncertainty treatment.

Third: review authority over AI-assisted work is being handed out by tenure and felt expertise, not audited outcomes. Linear’s mandatory second-review rate drops from 79% to 46% as tenure rises, and a widely circulated “calibration beats tenure” paper turns out to be written by the studied firm’s own executives on cohorts cut by the outcome they’re measuring. Mammography’s 25-year-old federal outcome audit never wired its results to review authority either — that link is unmeasured everywhere.
