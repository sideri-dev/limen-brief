A privacy control should be something you can check with your hand, not something you have to trust a menu about.

A within-subjects HCI study proposes PIPS — physical privacy controls that give "perceptible assurance" of sensor state through direct manipulation and default-safe deactivation — and backs it with three separate case studies: a physical webcam cover, a "Candid Mic," and an on-demand RFID reader.

A software mute toggle or a bare LED fails this test because its state isn't physically tethered to the capture hardware, so the design should default to automatic deactivation rather than something the user has to remember to check. It's kept past the usual 7-day window because it's a replicated design principle, not a version-pinned capability claim.

The channel you sync a robot arm to matters more than whether you sync it at all — and breath beats heart rate.

An HRI 2026 "embreathment" study found that syncing a teleoperated arm's movement to the user's own breathing, rather than an involuntary signal like heart rate or skin conductance, significantly increased felt body ownership and was the participants' preferred condition.

The finding names the specific lever: a user-controllable physiological signal, not an involuntary one, is what makes a remote or prosthetic body feel like yours.

Bio-mimetic haptics on a social robot make people like it more even as it gets creepier — those two reactions don't cancel each other out.

Glasgow researchers added heartbeat, purring, and thermal feedback to a PARO robot in a 42-person study and found the haptic layer significantly raised perceived warmth and likeability at the same time as it raised perceived eeriness.

That's a caution against reading rising "eeriness" scores during haptic prototyping as an automatic stop signal — the evidence here shows it can coexist with a net-positive reaction rather than override it.

How you structure oversight of a computer-use agent matters more than how much of it you add.

A 48-participant study comparing four oversight strategies for computer-use agents found that strategy, not the amount of human-in-the-loop checking, was what actually shaped whether users caught a problematic action — plan-based strategies specifically lowered the rate at which the agent's mistakes went unnoticed.

That argues against the reflexive fix of bolting on more checkpoints; the real lever is making the moment of decision legible.

Hybrid GUI-plus-agent interfaces make tasks less effortful, not faster — and who chooses to delegate has nothing to do with how risky the task is.

A 73-person HAI 2026 (Osaka) lab study comparing three interaction modes on a CRUD task found AI assistance cut clicks, navigation, and scrolling significantly without changing overall task duration, and that individual preference — not task risk — explained about half the variance in whether people used the assistant.

That's evidence against risk-tiered delegation gating as a design lever; a personalization-based default is the better one.

Yesterday's dossier

Backlog item #15 asked three questions about agent memory, and only one turns out to be a design question. What to surface is now measurable and asymmetric — a 4-month, 40-user randomized deployment found user-requested recall helps satisfaction while system-initiated recall doesn't, and mistimed callbacks actively hurt. What must stay editable or forgettable is a legal question with misaligned answers across regimes — the word "memory" appears zero times in the EU AI Act, while Japan's APPI defines the protected category by whether the operator can delete a record.

How to signal memory has no reliable answer: bare signalling reads as invasive across five studies, and the 25-year personalization-transparency record shows an inspectable profile mostly reassures rather than lets people meaningfully act. The actual undesigned surface is the write path — 96% of real ChatGPT memories are created unilaterally by the system, with confirm-before-persist and a diff view still unbuilt.
