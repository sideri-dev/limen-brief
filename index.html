1. A teleoperated “mirror robot” made strangers in a waiting room meet through its mirrors before they met face to face — and the design lesson is about knowing when to stop.

(single-source — primary paper unreachable; institutional account)

MirrorBot moved between preset positions until two strangers could see both themselves and each other in its mirrors, with no screen, no voice, no direct prompt. In the primary study (16 pairs), 12 of 16 groups reported their first meaningful contact happened through the mirrors, not face to face; a follow-up against a mirrorless robot, a wall mirror, and no device confirmed MirrorBot won specifically because of the eye contact it facilitated.

The catch: some participants found it “an overenthusiastic friend” — the paper’s real conclusion is that social technology needs to know not just how to initiate interaction but when to step back. A clean case of an indirect affordance beating a direct one, with its own failure mode built in. Research: Guo, Yu, Niu, Gao, Hoffman, Leshed and Green, ACM/IEEE HRI 2026 Design Track Best Paper.

2. Routing an LLM’s facilitation to the whole group, not to individuals privately, nearly doubled how often three-person teams reached the correct hiring decision.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

138 three-person groups (N=414) ran a classic hidden-profile hiring task — the kind where the correct candidate only emerges if everyone’s partial information gets shared — under three conditions: no help, an LLM messaging each person individually, or an LLM messaging the group as a whole. Both AI conditions helped, but the group-level version pushed correct-consensus from 32.6% to 56.5% and beat the individual version on disclosure and usability too, even though the LLM never knew which candidate was right.

For mixed-initiative group tools, this names the real design choice: the intervention’s target — individual or group — matters more than giving everyone their own assistant. Research: Córdova, Chiang, Schirmer, Yin and Horvát, arXiv:2611.19652.

3. When someone makes a mistake, a Socratic dialogue that walks them back to it beats a better explanation — and explanation quality barely matters at all.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A preregistered 400-person study compared four ways of responding to a learner’s error: just show the right answer, a generic explanation, a personalized LLM-written explanation, or a Socratic back-and-forth. The dialogue resolved 64% of errors and scored 0.24 SD higher on posttests than either explanation condition — personalization bought nothing over a generic explanation, and neither beat simply showing the answer.

It took almost twice as long but people rated it more interesting, less frustrating. The design lever for correction UIs isn’t explanation quality, it’s dialogue structure. Research: Asher, Cao and Carvalho, arXiv:2611.20061.

4. A home robot a parent partly operates themselves gets more parent-child talking than a fully autonomous one — at no cost in perceived effort.

(single-source — peer-reviewed journal, Japanese-original)

A Nagoya University study ran 12 parent-child pairs through three conditions — no robot, a fully autonomous robot, and a semi-autonomous one the parent partly drives — in a home-learning setting. The semi-autonomous condition significantly increased parent talk compared to the autonomous robot, without raising felt workload (some comparisons borderline; the sample is small at 12 dyads).

The design decision this names, generically, for home or educational companion robots: partial human operation can be the engagement lever full autonomy quietly removes. Research: Go, Kubota, Iwasaki, Sato and Ogawa, ヒューマンインタフェース学会論文誌 Vol.28 No.3.

5. A 337-designer survey finds AI-tool loyalty is narrow, and adoption drops by half once a project moves from defining the problem to building the solution.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Across Product, Industrial, and UX/UI designers in 40 countries, Industrial Designers used AI noticeably less (73.9% vs. 87–88% for the other two roles), and of 60 named AI tools, just 25 carried at least 90% of every role’s picks — loyalty concentrates hard on a handful of generalist tools.

Sharper still: predicted adoption among UX/UI designers fell from 87.2% in Define to 43.9% in Develop, a confirmed phase effect. For anyone advising a design team’s tool stack, especially across Japanese, Thai and English-language markets, this is the real adoption curve to plan around, not an assumed flat one. Research: Tajarmakan and Hojjati Emami, arXiv:2611.20519.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s daily-chain dossier dug into the “count vs. list” agent-tool paper that led the 2026-11-16 digest, and found the digest’s own framing needed correcting: the result is signal-versus-no-signal, not count-versus-list — a count alone stops only three specific newer models (and Gemini 3.1 Pro), while 36 earlier agents need a full listing to stop acting on an ambiguous record. The retail “quarter of wrong-account writes” figure actually comes from a lookup showing no signal at all, and the harm itself is a store-order artifact: an identical duplicate record sorted second produces zero wrong writes, sorted first it produces up to 23%.

Every tested fix carries a measured cost — listing leaks other customers’ data in 38.6% of completions, a hard policy line can tank legitimate task completion from 67/80 to 2/80. The underlying design rule (“disambiguate before acting”) isn’t new — Microsoft’s HAX guidelines stated it in 2019 — but this paper measures, for the first time, that agent compliance tracks the tool’s return shape rather than the model’s capability.
