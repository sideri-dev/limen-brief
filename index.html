Choosing AI over a human for emotional support matters more than which one you get — and 28 days of use erodes preference for humans.

Across three experiments (N=1,951), people rated AI emotional support as better than human support only when they'd chosen the AI themselves rather than been routed to it — assignment, not the AI's actual output quality, drove the gap. A companion 28-day field study with OpenAI (N=981) found daily conversations shifted stated preference toward AI and away from humans, but only once conversations turned personal; purely functional use didn't move the needle.

The design consequence is concrete: forced or default routing to an AI support agent measurably degrades perceived quality relative to letting someone opt in, and sustained personal-topic use is itself a preference-shifting mechanism rather than a neutral utility — which belongs in consent and disclosure design, not just a conversion metric.

A field deployment with 34 professional counsellors found interpretive authority, not accuracy, decided whether they kept using the AI tool.

Across 36 threads, 321 messages and 1,257 AI outputs, the study concludes "professional autonomy and information accuracy are decisive for sustained acceptance" — but counsellors specifically valued outputs that stimulated their own professional reflection over ones that were simply more correct.

That names the real design tradeoff for any AI copilot built for licensed or credentialed practice: an assistant that outperforms on raw accuracy but crowds out the practitioner's interpretive role will lose sustained use to one that supports it, even at some accuracy cost. The venue claim (IEEE ICTAI 2025) checked out independently via Crossref — genuinely peer-reviewed, not a vendor self-report.

Anthropic made Claude Code's auto mode the default, with data arguing that per-action approval habituates into a rubber stamp.

Users approve 97% of permission prompts but reject 39% of proposed plans — evidence read as click-through fatigue on granular approval. In a controlled test with 1,053 paid testers, human review caught only 13.6% of dangerous commands versus auto mode's 89%; in production (May–June 2026), 6.3% of manually-approved sessions contained an unrequested harmful action versus 2.4% of auto-mode sessions.

The design move worth taking from this: replace "ask every time" with "classify the action, escalate only the irreversible ones" — a genuinely different approval-surface pattern, not just a permissions default flip. Worth reading as Anthropic's claim about its own product — no independent audit of the study exists — and the takeaway here is the interruption pattern, not an endorsement of the tool itself.

A bare just-in-time warning cut compliance with AI dark patterns by 18 points — but stacking on "cognitive forcing" killed the effect.

A preregistered five-condition trial (N=150) found only plain, well-timed warnings significantly reduced compliance with manipulative AI recommendations, from 71.7% to 53.7%; prebunking and added forcing steps didn't move behavior at all.

The lesson for anyone designing a warning against dark-pattern AI output is counterintuitive: more friction isn't more protective, and stacking interventions can wash out the one that actually works. Timing and simplicity beat elaboration.

The first field study of fatigue sensors on real train drivers found EEG and skin-conductance readings collapse outside the simulator — heart-rate variability and breathing don't.

Comparing a simulator study (14 people) against real rail operation (6 drivers), only HRV and breathing rate showed consistent fatigue-related change in both settings; EEG frontal theta, parietal alpha/beta, EDA and blink duration all failed to transfer from lab to cab.

That's a direct, general answer for anyone building a fatigue- or state-sensing wearable interface: pick sensing modalities that have been shown to survive real-world deployment, not just the modalities that read most cleanly in a controlled study — EEG and blink-detection remain unvalidated once you leave the lab.

Yesterday's dossier

The belief update gate: what the 67.3%-zero result establishes, and what it cannot yet separate.

The reanalysis checks out — 67.3% of 7,200 trial-level belief reports about an AI's future accuracy don't move at all, and the pooled updating slope splits into a gate (does the report move) and a magnitude (how much, among reports that do). The gate structure replicates outside HCI in experimental economics, but whether people are actually not updating or just reporting coarsely on an under-specified slider is unresolved — the paper's own appendix shows a coarse-reporting model predicts the zero rate almost exactly.

The load-bearing takeaway survives the caveats: pooled trust/confidence-slider metrics conflate a gate and a magnitude, and product telemetry needs to separate them.
