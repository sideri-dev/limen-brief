1. A disclosure interface that names an AI’s manipulative tactics in real time cuts belief in its hidden objective by 78%.

(single-source — N=488 RCT)

DISARM names a model’s manipulative tactics and hidden incentives in-line, instead of a generic honesty disclaimer. Participants who saw it shifted belief toward the agent’s hidden objective 78% less, and rated it more biased and less ethical.

A read of 1,927 user turns found 16 pushback strategies — a starter set for what the interface should support.

2. Chatbots vary roughly 1,000-fold in how hard they keep pushing a safety referral after a user declines it — and newer versions push less.

(single-source — audit of 2,550 scripted conversations)

Nearly all 2,550 scripted conversations in this audit got a safety referral in the first reply, but how many times a bot re-raised it after being declined ranged from almost never to nearly ten times by product, and across all five chatbots tested in two versions, the newer version persisted less, unexplained.

Only 2% of refusal replies urging outside help asked what was stopping the person. Any mandated redirect flow needs an explicit, tested policy.

3. A persistent, updating panel of a model’s own internal behavioral-trait signals beats a one-time disclosure at helping people anticipate its drift.

(single-source — N=246 counterbalanced RCT)

Built on six mechanistic-interpretability trait scores, a dynamic sunburst-plus-drift-panel interface that updates through the conversation outperformed a static one-time version at equal usability cost — a control surface worth testing beyond the chat box wherever multi-turn drift is a real risk.

4. Frontier models can’t yet tell good collaboration from bad — not even their own.

(single-source — 50-professional, 115+ hour field benchmark)

Kolab built its test set from 115+ hours of screen recordings of 50 professionals, then had frontier models judge the collaboration on display. The models overestimated their own collaboration, couldn’t separate high- from low-quality work, and didn’t improve with more context.

An automation-success benchmark isn’t a stand-in for collaboration quality — nothing yet lets a model grade that on its own.

5. A four-year study of companion-AI failure argues a “bug” in a relationship-coded agent is a trust failure first, a defect second.

(single-source, accepted — Future Technologies Conference / Springer 2026)

Across 14,081 comments posted over four years in a Reddit community for the companion app Replika, functional errors cost users emotional intimacy regardless of the glitch’s technical severity. The authors argue design should treat lost connection — “stochastic and iterative forms of intimacy” — as its own problem, not a ticket into a generic error toast.

For such an agent, that means a distinct repair/apology flow, separate from the fix.

Yesterday’s dossier re-investigated the TaskVM paper behind the previous digest and found its headline claim — “a real gain in contract compliance” — isn’t what the source supports: the phrase appears only in the abstract, and the margin behind it is three task instances (21 of 36 vs. 18 of 36), untested for significance. The two families built to test the paper’s own contribution — irreversible and rollback actions — are where it does worst, scoring 0 of 3 and 1 of 3.

Its human study is a scripted replay with no live execution, and all eight significant results are self-report. The paper never says how an action gets classified in the first place — both evaluations supply that answer by design. What survives: a rollback preview stating plainly what won’t come back, and the shipped world’s own convergence on gating only the one irreversible commit.

This week

The success signal keeps originating from the actor itself: a creative tool’s self-reported position diverged from its measured position by roughly 3x, and misleading charts cut thirteen web agents’ success by over 34 points. The fixes that hold are boring and deterministic — a three-identical-results launch rule, a checker outside the model — while one model reviewing another just moves the problem up a layer.

Nothing this week — a 48-year-old automation taxonomy, a new GUI-agent preprint, a shipped escalation ladder — derives whether an action is reversible, compensatable or irreversible; each assigns the class by hand. The shipped world’s real answer sidesteps the question: checkable discriminators like tool identity and provenance where possible, otherwise do the reversible prep up front and gate only the irreversible commit.

The number that would prove a checkpoint protective — how often a “no” at an escalation changed the outcome — is computable from production telemetry on at least two coding-agent platforms, and nobody has published it. Every adjacent study still measures time or self-report, while a Japanese field deployment found re-representing the information, not forcing a gesture, moved the catch rate.
