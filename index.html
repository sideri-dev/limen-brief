Concept-based AI explanations only help decision-makers under specific conditions — not by default.

Two large studies (705 participants, nearly 7,000 observations) tested Concept Bottleneck Models — interfaces that expose the intermediate concepts an AI used to reach a decision — against plain AI output and against unaided human judgment. The benefit only showed up when the task was perceived as genuinely difficult, the underlying concepts were easy to identify, and the user could actively interact with the model; outside those conditions, an interactive concept-explanation layer bought nothing. Worse, when the model's concept detection was inaccurate, it undermined trust rather than building it.

That's a real build/skip decision for anyone weighing an interactive explanation surface, not a case for explainability in general.

A voice agent changes how people talk around it even when it never says a word.

A conversation-analytic study of human speech around an always-listening voice agent found that people shape what they say around the "ever-present risk" that any utterance might trigger an unwanted response — the agent's mere potential to speak becomes a constant, background constraint on human conversation, whether or not it ever activates.

This reframes barge-in design: the real decision isn't just when the agent should yield the floor, it's how the possibility of unwanted activation reshapes what people say around it in the first place. It's a durable method — conversation analysis — rarely applied to voice-agent design.

The worst AI persona isn't "too agreeable" — it's flattering and inconsistent.

A controlled 224-person study crossed two variables — whether an assistant complimented the user, and whether it adapted its stance to match theirs — and found the interaction, not either factor alone, drives trust. Complimentary AI that also flip-flopped its position reduced both perceived authenticity and trust; neutral AI that adapted its stance raised both.

That argues against the default instinct to make assistant personas "warm and agreeable": personality and consistency interact non-linearly with trust, and the worst-performing cell is exactly the one a lot of products default to.

The strongest argument for Anthropic's auto-mode-default has a specific hole: prompt injection.

Anthropic's data-backed case for defaulting Claude Code to a classifier-gated auto-approval mode — reflexive 97% human approval rates, a 1,053-tester study, and catch-rate numbers favoring the classifier over manual review — was covered here on 2026-08-25. Simon Willison's response, published the same week, doesn't dispute those numbers; he wants "more independent confirmation," specifically because a classifier trained to distinguish "dangerous command" from "safe command" may not generalize to prompt injection, a different threat class entirely.

His evidence: Anthropic's own internal sessions still run under heavy sandboxing, suggesting the company doesn't fully trust the classifier unsandboxed either. Anyone copying this pattern needs an answer to the injection case specifically, not just a good approval-rate number.

Tuning a proactive voice assistant is a per-driver optimization problem, not a single threshold.

A within-subjects VR study (19 participants) used Bayesian optimization to search for the best trade-off between an in-vehicle assistant's helpfulness and its cost in mental demand and predictability — run separately for each driver rather than against one fixed "how proactive" setting.

The result is a Pareto front of optimal trade-offs rather than a single number: a concrete research method for negotiating interruption cost against usefulness that generalizes past cars to any proactive assistant deciding when to speak up uninvited.

The 0.1/1/10-second latency triad everyone cites is a citation chain that lost its meaning at every link: Miller 1968 actually wrote seventeen task-relative categories he called "the best calculated guesses," and Shneiderman's oft-cited variability finding is repeated backwards — he measured that ±50% timing variation had "little impact on performance." The real counter-current is narrower than its retellings: two 2026 experiments show slower, visible "thinking" raises perceived warmth and thoughtfulness but not trust or competence, and can backfire — transparency on an unfavorable outcome scored worse than instant response. Conversational timing turns out to be a human constant, not a UI convention (120ms detection, 700–800ms reluctance), while 2026 serving reality makes it urgent: one model version spans 103× in time-to-first-token depending on its effort setting, so there's no stable median to design a loading state against.
