1. A 1.26-million-vote Chatbot Arena analysis explains why a model you haven’t touched can look like it’s getting worse: the evaluators are getting pickier, not the model.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Isolating fixed, unchanged model pairs across May 2023–August 2024, the “both are bad” vote share rose 1.71 points a month on a 17.1% mean (1.91 points a month in 2024 alone), and rose in all fourteen pairs tracked across both years — 15.5% to 23.3% — while the stronger model’s share of decisive votes stayed flat.

Before shipping a reactive fix for a falling satisfaction score on a model nobody’s changed, check whether the comparison itself has drifted; this is the first time that drift has a number attached to it.

2. A new turn-taking benchmark puts a real number on how good a duplex voice agent can ever get at knowing when to speak — and it’s a lot closer to one-in-three wrong than to human-level.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

The best model tested hits 66.5–67.9% accuracy predicting the right moment to take a turn, against a human ceiling of just 70.1% majority-vote agreement — and even that ceiling is capped by the same bottleneck a live system faces, since the human judges worked from clips no longer than 10 seconds and any cue inside the gap was lost to them too.

For anyone building duplex or voice-first interaction, this is a budget number: design for roughly one-in-three turn-transition misjudgments and a graceful recovery, not for near-human barge-in.

3. A disclaimer warning users about an AI’s political bias works — for about a week, and worst on exactly the people who need it most.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A preregistered study of 1,586 people found the disclaimer reduced persuasion, but the effect didn’t persist across contexts, and Republicans in the sample were both more persuaded and more trusting than Democrats regardless of condition. A separate finding in the same data: people who use AI more often in a given week showed higher trust and more persuasion afterward.

One-time disclaimers aren’t a durable fix; they need re-triggering, or a design that treats different audiences differently.

4. When researchers redesigned an AI code-review explanation, usability and actual remediation rates improved — but self-reported trust didn’t move at all.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A 24-person crossover study (after a 20-person pilot and a 19-practitioner workshop) found System Usability Scale scores rose from 77.3 to 85.0 and the odds of fixing the actual problem correctly were 2.6 times higher with the new explanation design — while trust, measured the normal way, was flat. The authors note a flat accuracy/time result elsewhere in the study is uninformative, not proof of equivalence.

The practical lesson: if you’re testing whether an explanation redesign worked, measure the downstream task outcome, not a trust survey — trust can sit still while everything that matters moves.

5. The same prompt can cost up to 13.7 times more tokens depending on what script it’s written in — a real, measurable budget tax that falls hardest on under-resourced languages.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

On GPT-4o’s tokenizer, the same content needs 1.57x the tokens for Hindi, 5.01x for Odia, and 13.7x for Santali written in Ol Chiki script, compared to English; GPT-4’s tokenizer is worse, up to 12.8x. Only one third-party tokenizer tested, Sarvam-30B/105B, stayed under 2.6x across every language measured.

Neither Japanese nor Thai was tested directly here, but the mechanism — non-Latin and under-resourced scripts costing disproportionately more to tokenize — generalizes, and it’s a real line item for anyone budgeting context windows or per-message cost across a multilingual practice.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s dossier re-read the Stanford Clio study behind the previous digest’s #1 item against the full 249,834-conversation sample and against Anthropic’s own published guidance for that exact dataset, and found four of its five headline percentages mean less than they appear to: against the full sample, “56% consequential” is 36.9%, “67% teaching” is 47.0%, and “72% human-led” is 70.6% — only the 49.7% friction figure holds roughly as stated. Anthropic’s own documentation for this dataset says accuracy figures “were measured on facets describing the topic of each conversation,” not on the judgment-style facets (teaching, friction, task stakes) the paper built its headlines from.

What survives is direction, not level: the dataset still shows stakes-labelled work is common, friction is mostly interactional rather than a model defect, and repair strategies differ sharply in outcome — but the claim that this bears directly on where to invest approval gates doesn’t survive, since the sample contains no gateable system action at all.
