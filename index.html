1. People who stay in a sustained AI-companionship relationship end up worse off — because it’s replacing human contact, not because the relationship itself is unhealthy.

(single-source — two-wave longitudinal study, 1,182 users)

A two-wave study surveyed 1,182 CharacterAI users at baseline and resurveyed 439 of them after a 12-month gap. Engagement held steady over that year, and sustained use was consistently tied to lower well-being — driven mainly by reduced in-person social contact (“social displacement”), not by engagement depth itself.

For any product sustaining long-term conversational engagement, that’s the real design lever: whether it crowds out human contact or leaves room for it.

2. Confidence is not a reliable signal that someone actually checked an AI agent’s output.

(single-source — N=12 formative + N=36 controlled study)

Researchers found users “accepted plausible outputs without thorough evaluation, and only recognized these lapses in retrospect.” A follow-on study of 36 people doing programming tasks, instrumented with gaze and physiological data, found people were “often highly confident while missing silent defects” — running only generic checks, never generating real diagnostic evidence.

An oversight UI for agentic tools needs to force diagnostic evidence-gathering on anything plausible-looking, not just surface a confidence score.

3. Letting an AI advisor say “I don’t know” helps confident users trust it more, and hurts unsure users’ trust in it.

(single-source — preregistered experiment, N=455)

A preregistered experiment gave an AI advisor the option to abstain instead of “always provide a response.” High-expertise users’ acceptance rose from 38.2% to 53.3% when abstention was available; low-expertise users’ fell from 73.4% to 68.1% — a genuine crossover, mediated by the added cognitive burden of handling “I don’t know.”

A single abstention policy across a whole user base is the wrong default — it’s a segmentation call.

4. Notion dropped pure LLM-as-judge scoring, decided full pairwise comparison doesn’t scale, and calibrated a pointwise judge against pairwise results instead.

(vendor-primary, single-source — Notion’s own account)

Its own writeup on model router Auto: “simply asking an LLM to score the outputs was not reliable enough,” while full pairwise/Elo comparison “would have been both too slow and too costly” to run daily. The calibrated pointwise judge agreed with pairwise rankings “more than 80% of the time,” and Auto now hits “roughly 90% of the performance of frontier models… for about a 90% lower cost” (5/10/25 credits vs. Opus 5.5’s 50/85/150).

The reusable pattern for any model router or quality gate: pointwise scoring calibrated against pairwise ground truth, not either alone.

5. The dead time while an agent works on a multi-minute task doesn’t need a spinner — it needs something to do.

(single-source — N=12 formative + N=16 within-subjects)

A study of 12 daily agent users found “staying felt like wasted time, while switching away fragmented attention and forfeited the context needed to re-engage.” The fix, Sidecar, is a same-surface panel that “reads the run and surfaces implicit decisions the agent made” while it’s still working.

In a 16-person study it helped people “stay focused on their main task… and recognize under-specification in their own prompts” — catching their own bad instructions before the agent finished acting on them.

Yesterday’s dossier

Deep-checking yesterday’s #1 item found the EDPB’s smart-glasses statement is weaker than it reads: non-binding “preliminary reflections,” adopted without consultation, with no scheduled follow-up. Its central clause — no indicator coverable without stopping capture — already fights the capture mode the market is moving toward, since at least one major wearable maker runs its AI-assistance camera continuously with the indicator light off by design.

The claim that today’s indicators leave bystanders unaware cites no detection study, only preference surveys, and two of its supporting details — a Japan ordinance, a California bill — don’t check out under a primary-source read. The statement’s own text places the disclosure duty on “the controller,” not makers.
