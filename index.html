Two-thirds of the time, a user isn't updating their trust at all — and a slider can't tell you that.

A reanalysis of a preregistered CHI 2026 dataset (240 participants, 7,200 trials) finds most trial-level belief changes are exactly zero (67.3%) or nearly so (76.4% under five points), and the two kinds of movement run on different signals: whether feedback disagrees with belief predicts if a person updates at all, direction predicts how much and which way — but only among the third of trials that move.

That kills a quiet assumption behind most trust-calibration instrumentation: a continuous slider or check-in survey treats every reading as movement, and pooling non-movers with movers washes out the real signal. Any in-product trust measurement needs a gate that asks whether the user updated before interpreting how much.

Anthropic's new "Concise" output style draws a hard, citable line: shrink the narration, never the safety-relevant content.

Claude Code's docs describe a style that "leads with the result, skips preamble and narration, and keeps responses short by default" while doing the same underlying work — but explicitly refuses to compress "error reports, security warnings, and confirmations for destructive actions."

The exact ship date can't be pinned down (Anthropic's changelog carries no per-version dates), so read this as recent rather than dated to a specific day. It's a directly reusable pattern for approval or confirmation UI in an agentic tool: verbosity is a dial, but there's a fixed floor of content that never shortens.

A voice model just posted sub-90ms latency — low enough to support real interruption, not just fast turn-taking.

Cartesia's Sonic-3.6, paired with its Ink-2 transcription model, claims "sub-90ms TTS and 100ms transcript latency with native turn detection," and Artificial Analysis's independent leaderboard puts it #1 on both its speech arenas, ahead of Speechify's Simba 3.2 and Alibaba's Qwen-Audio-3.0-TTS-Plus.

That matters because it sits below the roughly 200ms threshold this lane treats as the line between "the system responds" and "the system can be talked over" — a voice interface that takes turns versus one that supports genuine barge-in.

Warm, empathetic chatbot design can inflate trust independent of whether the system is actually safe — and mental-health AI is where that gap shows up first.

A 61-paper survey proposes separating trust into three layers — how much a person trusts the system, how trustworthy the interaction itself is, and how trustworthy the underlying AI is — and argues the field has been optimizing the wrong one: "empathetic chatbots can elicit strong user trust without commensurate safety, while safer systems are under-trusted when their boundaries are opaque."

For any high-stakes advisory product, that's a design-team-level split worth adopting directly: warm copy and safety-boundary disclosure are two separate levers, and only one of them should move the trust number. The empirical base here is mental-health specific — treat the framework, not the numbers, as portable elsewhere.

Perceived intention, not a face: what the HAI 2026 gaze-cueing result actually establishes.

Corrects yesterday's #1 digest item on the record: the underlying paper was presented February 28, 2026, not August, and the venue explicitly carries no peer review — the digest's "peer-reviewed experimental result" framing was wrong. The gaze-cueing mechanism itself is fourteen years old; what's genuinely new is inducing the belief via a witnessed demonstration rather than an instruction, and doing it with a faceless cue-giver.

The manipulation is confounded (exposure to the physical robot isn't separated from the capability demonstration), and the paper's proposed use — gaze-following as a covert probe of user stance — runs into a known dissociation between implicit gaze measures and self-reported trust.
