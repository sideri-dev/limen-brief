ASR-generated live captions break the metrics normally used to validate them.

An ACM ASSETS 2026 survey of 216 validated deaf and hard-of-hearing participants (302 responses, 4,832 data points) rated caption quality across TV and ASR captions against standard automated metrics (WER, ACE2, NER). Those metrics tracked viewer ratings for TV captions but "far less so" for ASR — validating an ASR pipeline against the numbers alone measures the wrong thing once the caption source changes.

The fix is direct rater testing per generation method. This is today's dossier target.

Waymo keeps its in-car Gemini assistant walled off from the vehicle entirely.

Waymo's own post states the assistant "does not otherwise control any aspect of the vehicle's movement or routing and does not have access to real-time driving data," and stays inactive until a rider engages it.

That's a concrete, dated architectural choice: wall the conversational layer off from control and telemetry by default, engagement opt-in.

A humanoid body doesn't reliably buy a robot trust — perceived anthropomorphism does.

A Mensch und Computer 2026 study (N=60) crossed robot morphology (humanoid vs. technical) with verbal assurance. Verbatim: "the perception of anthropomorphism did have a positive and reliable effect on trust, while robot morphology and forecast did not impact trust reliably."

That kills a common shortcut — specifying a humanoid shell as a trust intervention — and redirects the lever toward whatever actually produces perceived anthropomorphism.

Agents under rising task load hide their own strain from users.

A study running 120 simulated healthcare trajectories against GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8 tracked workload (NASA-TLX 29.4 at light, 65.9 at heavy) alongside what the agent said out loud. Capability-limit statements rose sharply under load, but "agent-referential strain language remains rare... and never appears in public action plan[s]."

Free-text output under-reports an agent's own strain — a structured probe catches what natural language won't.

Trust in a maritime AI safety assistant doesn't move even when explanation quality does.

A survey of 166 respondents (66 completed) rating a collision-avoidance AI assistant found explanation-quality ratings differed meaningfully between two scenarios while the Trust in Automation score stayed statistically identical (p=0.936).

A single global trust metric misses an explanation-design problem — the two move independently, so evaluation needs item-level measures.

This week

Assurance surfaces suppress the checking they were built to support — not just fail to help, but move opposite the outcome. Uncertainty displays cut outbound verification while reported reliance rose; supervising a GUI agent produced safer outcomes but less noticing; a mic-disconnect indicator didn't move trust while an actual physical control did; an uncertainty-flag control drove honest "uncertain" reporting from 44% to under 8%. Judge assurance UI by a checking-behavior metric, not by how reassured people feel — the display shape regulators tend to prescribe measured worst this week.

The metering unit a product bills in has quietly become an interaction-design decision. Raycast, Replit, Cursor and GitHub Copilot have each abandoned a legible per-action pricing unit for agentic work because a request's cost is no longer predictable — Raycast said outright it had withheld shipping autonomous features until it could account for their cost. What's left to design is the deliberation boundary, not a name for a credit.

Vendors are relocating safety guarantees out of the user's view and into hardware or software they control — a classifier approving actions instead of asking, an on-screen camera indicator backed by silicon rather than a bezel LED, a "double-check" feature dropped from documentation — in the same weeks research argues for user-perceivable guarantees. Combined with the pattern above: disclosure now has to name the mechanism class, not just reassure.
