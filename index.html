An activity-tracking app's own "not sure" suggestion talks people out of admitting they're not sure.

A peer-reviewed IMWUT study paired an auto-suggest labeling feature with an explicit uncertainty-annotation control and found honest "uncertain" tags collapse from 44.1% to 7.6% once the system starts guessing. In a 7-day free-living deployment, only 1.5% of 93,905 annotated minutes got marked uncertain at all, and just 5 of 14 participants said they used the control regularly — the paper's own diagnosis is that pairing a suggestion with an uncertainty affordance breeds false confidence rather than honest calibration.

Any product putting an AI guess next to a "not sure" button needs to test whether the guess is quietly switching the button off.

What makes a reviewer actually catch an LLM's mistake isn't a better explanation — it's whether the reasoning is retrievable later.

Two randomized experiments with 640 customer-facing employees found self-generated explanations (the reviewer writes their own reasoning first) improved error detection and recall, and a later cue that reactivated that reasoning kept detection working under repeated use. Richer AI-authored explanations did neither job.

That names the actual design lever for an oversight surface: an onboarding step where the human explains their own check, then a later prompt that brings the reasoning back, rather than a fancier explanation panel. Single-source preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.

Anthropic's commerce-agent architecture never lets the model itself move money — and it rejected custom UI markup for a plain engineering reason.

The harness, not the model, controls every write: payments, refunds, price changes and campaign launches all pass through a call that only succeeds for actions already approved on a real human-facing surface — a portal button, a CLI confirmation, a tool-approval prompt. Anthropic also tried and dropped custom XML-style UI tags for agent-built interfaces, because tag definitions living in the system prompt meant every new component bloated context and every edit risked regressions elsewhere.

The maker-checker split, not model alignment, is the load-bearing safety mechanism here, and the UI-markup rejection is a concrete argument against a pattern a lot of teams are currently building.

Zed concluded no instruction can stop an agent that really wants to touch your files — so it moved enforcement to the OS.

Its own reasoning: "a rule that bans git .* does very little to prevent an agent that really wants to modify your .git folder," and prompt-level rules do even less against prompt injection. Sandboxing now runs through Seatbelt on macOS, Bubblewrap namespaces on Linux, and WSL on Windows, because "agents cannot be trusted to determine whether an action is something the user would want."

It's a durable line — prompt-level guardrails versus enforcement-level ones — independent of which model is asking.

Vercel stopped trying to keep agent credentials secret and started making them short-lived instead.

Their argument: a vaulted long-lived token "never expires, and no vault limits what a leaked credential can do," and agents make that worse by touching more systems, more autonomously, more often, while the tooling to contain a secret hasn't changed. Vercel Connect replaces the vault with per-deployment OIDC identity and auto-scoped, expiring, per-task tokens.

Credential design for agents is a scope-and-lifetime problem, not a secrecy problem — that's the axis to design against.

Yesterday's dossier

Yesterday's #1 (Chinese smart-home bystander-privacy audit) turns out to be the weakest reading of a converging finding, not a China-specific one: two other bystander-consent audits, in different countries and years, found the identical absolute zero. The gap is structural, not negligent — every shipped bystander-consent mechanism (Meet, Call Recording) works because the bystander is reachable and can leave; a visitor at a smart-home's front door has neither property.

Almost no law actually requires the missing screen inside a private home, and the one controlled study that tested whether people even want it (N=761) found no significant owner/bystander difference. The corrected read: don't ship the missing consent screen — decide what the device refuses to do when the person in front of it isn't addressable.
