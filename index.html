1. Uncertainty displays don’t just add caution — their granularity decides which direction behavior moves.

(single-source — accepted, ACM FAccT ’26)

A 192-person randomized trial on medical questions tested three ways of showing an LLM’s uncertainty and found they don’t converge on “more transparency, more scrutiny.” Token-level uncertainty — marking individual uncertain words — actually raised agreement with the AI’s answer (p≤.05, odds ratio 1.62) rather than prompting more checking.

Output- and relation-level uncertainty didn’t move agreement, but did measurably cut people’s confidence in their own judgment, and the relation-level version also raised self-reported reliance on the AI — one participant skipped verification because the tool “summarized all key information.” Strong enough to upend the default single confidence-badge pattern: how granular an uncertainty display is matters more than whether one exists, and the obvious choice can backfire.

2. A clarifying question buys faster acceptance, not more careful review — especially on the thing it just asked about.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 31 2026)

A 180-person study across six decision scenarios found that when an AI asks a clarifying question before answering, people review the eventual response about a third faster (55 vs. 81 seconds) and accept it unchanged more often (60% vs. 46%). The effect holds even when the response is wrong: people agreed with incorrect answers more often after a clarifying question, and agreement climbed further specifically when the error sat next to the thing the question had asked about.

Uncomfortable for a popular design instinct — a clarifying question reads as careful collaboration but functions as a satisfaction shortcut, so content adjacent to it needs its own check, not less scrutiny.

3. Anthropic is moving agent conversation state server-side — and naming the tradeoffs up front.

(vendor-primary, single-source — Anthropic docs, Nov 2 2026)

A new beta feature called “message threads” lets a long-running agent stop re-uploading and re-tokenizing its entire growing conversation on every turn; the server keeps the state and the client sends only what’s new. Anthropic is unusually blunt about the cost: a thread is best-effort, expiring five minutes after its last use and deleted within an hour regardless, with no Zero Data Retention or HIPAA eligibility.

Their own advice to builders: keep an independent copy of every message and build a replay path from day one. A real latency lever for any growing-context agent interface, but one with a session-loss and compliance tradeoff to design around, not assume away.

4. Agent failure modes don’t change with autonomy level — what a person can do about them does.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 30 2026)

A taxonomy built from 695 real human-agent interaction incidents found all twelve identified failure modes show up whether an agent operates with tight oversight or wide autonomy; autonomy changes the shape a failure takes, not whether it happens. What actually shifts with configuration is recoverability: how much an agent completes between points of human involvement is fixed at deployment, so identical models on identical tasks fail differently depending only on how checkpoints are spaced.

That reframes “how autonomous should this be” from a risk question into a recovery-design question — checkpoint placement, not model trust, determines what a person can do when something goes wrong.

5. A teleoperation wearable restores the feeling of control over a detached robotic arm — but not the feeling that the arm is yours.

(single-source — accepted, IEEE-RAS Humanoids 2026)

MESSPa, a wearable interface for operating a robot arm that can detach from the body, significantly cut task time and restored “sense of agency” versus keyboard control once the arm detached — closing much of the gap between attached and detached performance. What didn’t move was “sense of ownership,” the felt sense the arm belongs to you — that stayed flat regardless of interface.

Useful split for remote-embodiment design generally: motion-transfer plus positional haptic feedback recovers functional control, but that’s not the same lever as whatever would make a detached limb feel like part of you.
