1. A feedback button after a wrong AI answer repairs trust — but never fully, and only if the next answer is right.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — N=1,035 across five experiments)

A study in the Journal of the Association for Information Systems found that once an LLM-based assistant gives wrong information, user trust drops, and giving people a way to flag it only partially brings trust back — “not to baseline levels.” The repair only holds if the system’s follow-up answers are actually accurate; a second bad regenerated answer taints perceived accuracy on later tasks, even unrelated ones, rather than resetting the relationship.

For anyone designing a “thumbs down” or correction surface, this is the real bar: it’s not a trust-restoring gesture on its own, it’s a conditional one, gated entirely on whether the system gets it right next time.

2. LLM-written pedestrian signals for self-driving cars are understandable but don’t make people act correctly.

(single-source, peer-reviewed, ACM IMWUT/UbiComp 2026)

A 26-participant field study compared human-designed external vehicle signals against ones a GPT-o3-mini generated. People interpreted the AI-written signals about as well as the human-designed ones — but that didn’t translate into pedestrians behaving correctly. The authors are explicit: correct interpretation and correct reaction are not the same test.

That’s a direct warning against validating a redesigned external vehicle signal purely on comprehension surveys; it needs a behavioral test too.

3. A hearable can borrow other people’s microphones by tracking who’s actually facing whom.

(single-source, peer-reviewed, ACM IMWUT/UbiComp 2026)

CoHear groups nearby hearable-device wearers into a shared processing unit based on head orientation — who is actually in conversation with whom — rather than relying on dedicated shared infrastructure. Grouping hits over 90% accuracy, speech quality improves by up to 8.8 dB over prior baselines, and a 20-person usability study scored it well above baseline.

The design implication: the unit of audio processing for a hearable should be the conversation, not the single wearer — which changes what a hearable’s mic array needs to coordinate with across devices.

4. A car’s wireless phone charger can double as a gesture sensor — no camera needed.

(single-source, peer-reviewed, ACM IMWUT/UbiComp 2026)

MagHarp repurposes the magnetic field of an ordinary in-car wireless charging pad to recognize hand gestures, hitting 97.6% accuracy across a 3,500-sample dataset, tested on 5 chargers and 4 phones, effective to 20cm.

It’s a concrete alternative to camera- or IR-based in-car gesture systems, trading a bit of range for avoiding the privacy and cost exposure of adding a camera array.

5. A new attention method cuts long-context LLM response time by 3.4x.

(single-source, peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2026, venue-checked)

Prism Attention exploits the fact that a model’s most-attended tokens stay stable across layers even when the exact ranked list shifts, letting it share attention indices across layers instead of recomputing them. The payoff: a 3.4x end-to-end decoding speedup over FlashAttention at 128K context length.

That’s a real number for deciding whether a long-context feature can be served live in a conversation or needs to be designed as an async, batch-style operation instead.
