A CHI 2026 study finally answers how often an agent should stop and ask — not just whether it should.

Researchers ran a formative study that surfaced a recurring failure pattern (confirm, diagnose, correct, redo) in multi-step agent tasks, then built a decision-theoretic model for where to place confirmation checkpoints and tested it head-to-head against the standard confirm-only-at-the-end approach with 48 participants. 81% preferred the intermediate-confirmation model, and it cut task completion time 13.54%.

That's a measured alternative to the binary approve/auto-run choice most agent products still ship — checkpoint interval and placement turn out to be design variables, not an afterthought.

Only one explanation format actually helps people tell a correct AI answer from a wrong one — and it isn't any of the three currently shipped as "transparency."

A 192-person between-subjects study found reasoning traces and post-hoc explanations are persuasive but not informative: they raise acceptance of an LLM's output regardless of whether that output is right. Dual explanation — presenting the case for and against a prediction together — was the only condition that genuinely improved people's ability to catch errors.

Worth testing against whatever explanation UI is currently shipping under the trust-building banner. Single-source preprint.

An oversight redesign made people faster and more confident — and no more accurate.

Microsoft Research ran three user studies on a computer-use agent and found their redesigned oversight interface cut the time participants spent hunting for errors and raised their confidence in their own decisions, but final accuracy was not meaningfully improved.

That's a direct warning against the obvious success metric for an oversight surface: confidence and speed can both go up while the thing the interface exists to do — catching real errors — stays flat.

Developers reviewing agent-generated code have converged on three shortcuts that feel like verification and aren't.

Interviews with 17 developers (13 with 7+ years' experience) at an ACM FAccT 2026 venue found working engineers treat an agent's stated plan as a faithful proxy for what it actually did, treat passing tests as proof the code is correct, and treat a quick eyeball of agent output as a reliable issue-signal — three heuristics that substitute for real checking.

That names the exact UI elements — diff view, plan display, test pass/fail — that function as trust shortcuts rather than checks, useful for anyone designing what an oversight surface actually shows.

Letting an LLM score its own robot's social performance works for engagement — and gets comfort backwards.

A RO-MAN 2026 paper had five LLMs complete standard human-robot-interaction questionnaires as the robot, compared against 25 human-rated interactions and replicated live on a Claude Sonnet 4.5-powered Nao robot. Satisfaction and enjoyment scores correlated well with human ratings (r up to .72), but the comfort/strangeness dimension came out systematically inverted.

A team automating embodied-agent evaluation can trust the LLM for engagement metrics but needs a human in the loop for anything about discomfort or uncanny-valley reactions.

Yesterday's dossier

Yesterday's #1 (the activity-tracker uncertainty-suppression study) mostly holds up under deep re-checking, but the headline number doesn't mean quite what it looked like. Measuring the paper's own figures directly, the dossier finds the real per-annotation suppression from pairing an AI suggestion with an uncertainty control is roughly 2.7x, not the ~5.8x the reported 44.1%-to-7.6% figure implies — the gap is a segmentation artifact of how the system pre-chunks the timeline, not double the effect.

It also surfaces the paper's real, unstated thesis: the suggestion and the human "uncertain" control were answering the same underlying question (when something happened), so they were competing rather than one simply suppressing the other. The suppression direction itself replicates independently at much larger scale (N=3,132) in a separate preprint. No design vocabulary exists anywhere in current UX guidance (Apple, Google PAIR, Microsoft HAX, NIST) for this failure mode — user-side uncertainty, as opposed to the system's own confidence display.
