The grain of an uncertainty display, not just its presence, decides whether people verify an AI's answer or defer to it.

A 192-person FAccT 2026 study tested three ways of showing model uncertainty in medical Q&A. Token-level highlighting increased agreement with the AI. Output- and relation-level uncertainty didn't increase agreement — instead it quietly eroded people's confidence in their own answers, and relation-level display specifically suppressed independent verification (searches, checking sources).

A confidence badge and a highlighted-phrase treatment are not the same intervention and need separate evaluation.

Months of daily use don't teach people which parts of their voice assistant are the device and which are a third party.

A CHI 2026 longitudinal study of five older adults found participants who reported little worry about their voice-first ambient AI still couldn't say who could access their data or distinguish built-in function from third-party app access — even after extended use.

One-time settings screens don't fix this; it argues for a persistent, queryable "who can hear this" surface instead.

Showing people the evidence behind a claim beats stamping it "Made with AI."

An 81-person study out of the University of Tokyo (with Jun Rekimoto) compared an idealized "Provenance Density" interface — surfacing evidence and a consistency check — against a plain no-signal control, finding a large gap in people's ability to tell truth from fabrication (d=1.82) where the no-signal group showed none at all.

It's a lab result on an idealized interface, not a shipped product, but it's a direct, citable argument against binary AI-content labels as sufficient transparency.

Not telling people what a sensor is doing doesn't make them comply — it makes them over-restrict themselves defensively.

A qualitative study of 24 people under mandatory electronic monitoring in China found that where participants had mapped out how the system actually behaved, they regained some flexibility; where the system stayed opaque, they contracted their movement and activity well beyond what the rules required, and some kept those habits after the device came off.

The lesson generalizes past this device: undisclosed consequential sensing produces defensive behavior the system itself never sees.

Replit stopped stuffing agent instructions into one static system prompt — it now injects each rule only at the moment it's needed.

Their own writeup: stacking reminders in a system prompt causes compliance to collapse once you pass a handful at once, because the model falls back to pretrained habits when rules get verbose or conflicting. Injecting guidance at the decision point via a classifier fixed it at 90% lower cost than dynamically rewriting the prompt.

The actual lever is injection timing plus a hard cap on simultaneous reminders — not "more prompt" or "less prompt."
