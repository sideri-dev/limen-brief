1. A peer-reviewed conversation-analysis study finds the reason voice agents feel intrusive hasn’t changed since before LLMs — it’s structural, not a recognition bug.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — ACM TOCHI 33(5), Article 46, 2026)

Rudaz, Broth and Mlynar’s ACM TOCHI paper names “omnirelevance of speech” — the standing possibility that any utterance might wrongly trigger the agent — as constitutive of talking to a voice agent, present in both pre-LLM rule-based robots and today’s LLM systems. People manage this risk constantly, not just when the agent mis-fires.

The finding argues for designed conversational off-ramps — ways to visibly take a turn out of the agent’s address — rather than treating this as an ASR accuracy gap a bigger model eventually closes.

2. A two-year telemetry study of 800 developers finds AI coding assistants change behavior sharply while developers’ own sense of what changed barely moves.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — ICSE 2026)

Logged activity: “AI users produce substantially more code but also delete significantly more” — a churn pattern invisible in a parallel 62-professional survey, where “respondents report productivity gains and perceive minimal changes in other dimensions.”

The gap between what the logs show and what people report about their own work is the finding, not either number alone. For anyone building an AI-adoption rollout dashboard: self-report measures something different from what’s happening in the codebase, and treating it as sufficient hides the churn.

3. A new training method for full-duplex voice models fixes the exact ambiguous moment current systems can’t learn from: is this a real interruption, or just a backchannel?

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 26 2026)

The Window Likelihood method beats binary-reward GRPO training across tested models — MiniCPM-o rises from 83.9 to 89.6, PersonaPlex from 52.4 to 77.2 — by giving a learning signal in the windows today’s binary reward gives none, the paper’s “zero-gradient trap.” The comparison isn’t fully controlled for training data against Kyutai’s released checkpoints — treat it as a lead, not a settled benchmark.

For duplex voice interfaces this names the barge-in problem precisely: the model has no signal for exactly the moments where interruption-vs-backchannel matters most.

4. A six-month matched-cohort survey finds developers reporting a worsened experience nearly doubled even as perceived productivity held flat — not a tradeoff, a simultaneous rise.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted May 22 2026)

The share reporting a worsened experience went from 14% to 27%, while 84% still reported improvement on productivity at both measurement points. 82% report “less… on writing code” specifically (correcting a draft paraphrase; the underlying number is unchanged).

The authors propose “supervisory engineering work” as the shape the role is taking — a productivity metric moving the right way is not evidence the tool is working for the person using it.

5. Letting people iteratively edit their own “AI clone” makes them rate it far better — and does almost nothing for the actual visitor talking to it.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — ACM CUI ’26, Best Paper Award)

Sony CSL’s field deployment found source users’ ratings of their own AI clone improved significantly on nine of ten communication dimensions after iterative self-editing, while visitor ratings “showed no overall change and reached significance for only one of six AICs after FDR correction.”

The asymmetry is clean: self-editing moves how the creator feels about their own representation, not how well it lands with the person on the other side of the conversation — a caution for any self-personalized agent design that treats the creator’s satisfaction as a stand-in for the counterpart’s experience.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s #1 item (Google AI Overviews grounding study) comes apart under full-text reading: the headline null — “grounding unrelated to accuracy” — reverses into a significant positive correlation in the one subsample the paper calls closest to real search queries. The quoted “56.7%” figure uses a narrower denominator than it reads and carries a 23-point confidence interval the abstract omits; across comparable audits, support rates order by how permissive the measurement is, not by product, so the number isn’t portable into a client room as-is.

Facebook.com, the fourth-most-cited AI Overview domain in the paper’s own released data, is missing from its source table because of a readability gate — a direction that flatters the paper, not against it. Most importantly, the design conclusion that citation display doesn’t earn trust is contradicted at its decisive step by a pre-registered CHI ’25 study finding real, relevant, accurate sources do reduce overreliance on wrong answers — this paper is evidence the sourcing condition failed here, not that showing sources is the wrong design.
