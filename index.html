1. A peripheral status display cuts delegated-task errors nearly in half without costing extra attention.

(single-source — ACM UIST 2026, arXiv preprint)

Michigan and Adobe Research built Sidekick: an ambient green/yellow/orange/red indicator for a computer-use agent, with auto-pause at a risk threshold and a replay-based summary on return. In a 30-participant study juggling arithmetic against a delegated spreadsheet task, people using Sidekick averaged 1.31 errors versus 2.51 with a chat baseline and 2.32 with plain peripheral text (p=.001 on total score vs. peripheral text) — without extra monitoring time or cost to their own task.

It’s the most complete affordance set this corpus has seen for the standing agent-progress problem — color state, a pause rule, a resumption summary, measured together. Presented at UIST 2026 in Detroit.

2. One correct approval can roughly halve an on-device agent’s safety rate on the very next decision.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A new paper names “correct-precedent generalization”: once a local AI agent correctly releases one item whose restriction has genuinely been lifted, it becomes more willing to release the next item that’s still restricted — nothing wrong in the history explains it. The effect held across 13 open-weight models from nine developers; for the worst-affected model, one correct precedent roughly halved its safe rate. The authors’ fix, Paired Decision Distillation, brought every distilled model back to near-100% safe.

The lesson for an approval-history interface: letting an agent or a user treat “it got this right before” as standing trust is building on sand — that trust doesn’t transfer between similar-looking decisions.

3. Showing a model’s reasoning makes an answer feel better, not more correct — and people can’t tell the difference.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, Microsoft Research)

A preregistered study (N=559, ten LSAT-style problems) compared no reasoning trace, a full trace, and a short summary of one. Summaries held accuracy at the no-trace baseline while still raising trust and likability; full traces, under a weaker open-weight model, hurt performance outright. Across every condition people overestimated how well they’d done, and the overestimation tracked how good the trace felt, not how much they trusted it.

“Show the chain-of-thought so people calibrate appropriately” is not a safe default — a reasoning-trace surface needs its own accuracy test before it earns that justification.

4. Replit’s own numbers kill “more agents is always better.”

(single-source — Replit, vendor benchmark)

Replit benchmarked a single coding agent against swarms of 8, 16 and 32 agents across six frontier models on two-hour optimization tasks. A 32-agent Opus swarm reached 18.9x single-agent speed; the same swarm size running GPT-6.2 Astra got worse as it grew, because the coordinator became the bottleneck.

The swarms that scaled worked off a shared board and barely messaged each other directly (1.4 messages per Opus worker, versus 20-22 for the struggling swarms). Whether to expose a “swarm mode” is a question about that model’s coordination behavior, not a budget dial.

5. A small Thai OCR model fixes its own repetition-loop bug and jumps two Thai benchmarks while barely moving on English.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Typhoon OCR 2 ships compact 2B and 0.8B models; the 2B model’s repetition-loop failure rate drops from 3.2% to 0.7%, Thai MDPBench rises from 68.9 to 80.7, and ThaiOCRBench from 40.45 to 59.91, while English olmOCR-bench barely moves (84.4 to 84.7). The 0.8B model gets a smaller, statistically uncertain version of the same gain — the submitting researcher’s headline had conflated the two sizes, corrected here.

Small enough to run on-device, with a named and measured failure fixed, this bears on whether a Thai-market document-capture flow can stay local rather than round-tripping to the cloud.
