1. A three-month study puts interface redesign up against the actual job of overseeing an AI — and redesign loses.

(single-source, preprint — N=8, 12 weekly meetings)

Eight tutors spent three months overseeing an AI-supported learning assistant while researchers ran action research alongside them: 12 weekly meetings, two workshop iterations, four questionnaires. The team identified stimulation, mastery, and relatedness — not UI friction — as the real drivers of oversight disengagement, and the targeted UI changes they shipped in response had, in their own words, “mixed success.”

The lesson for anyone building an oversight dashboard: test whether the problem is motivational and job-design before spending another cycle on layout.

2. A trained auditor catches 12 of 20 hidden policy violations in a tool-using agent. Random probing catches 1. An LLM judge can’t tell the difference.

(single-source, peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2026)

Researchers hid 20 policies inside a frozen Qwen3-30B-A3B agent running 8 tools, then tested how well different auditing strategies found them. Random probing surfaced just 1; a small trained auditor (Qwen3-4B, fine-tuned via QLoRA) found 12, with multi-policy failures showing up in nearly every evaluation episode.

Worse for anyone hoping to automate the auditing itself: an LLM judge agreed with its own repeated verdicts almost perfectly but matched actual human raters only 40% of the time. Passive red-teaming badly undercounts real violations, and a judge model is not a safe substitute for a human reviewer.

3. Giving an autonomous-vehicle supervisor a haptic nudge raises situational awareness for free — and aerial oversight is measurably harder than ground oversight on every count.

(single-source, preprint — within-subjects)

Across modality conditions, haptic and visual-haptic feedback significantly improved situational awareness over visual-only feedback, with no added workload cost. Separately, the same study found aerial supervision carries a significantly bigger workload penalty than ground supervision on every measure tested.

For a hazard-monitoring interface, that’s a case for adding a haptic channel instead of another visual alert, and a flag that aerial and ground supervision need their own workload budgets rather than one shared design.

4. Nielsen Norman Group ran its own synthetic-vs-human respondent study, and synthetic data got the headline right while quietly erasing the variation that makes user research useful.

(single-source — primary report of NN/G’s own study, N=604 human / 120 synthetic)

Posing the same question to 604 humans and 120 synthetic respondents, NN/G found synthetic answers collapsed onto about a dozen labels where humans spread across more than 50, and synthetic respondents used UX jargon at a significantly higher rate than humans — a gap that widened sharply for one subgroup, product managers (93% synthetic vs. 44% human).

The top-line direction matched; the texture that a research method is supposed to surface didn’t. This is now the citable argument against swapping a synthetic panel in for real recruitment in a client-facing research plan.

5. Letting someone feel and correct an AI’s guess at a material’s properties nearly doubles its accuracy, in about six and a half minutes.

(single-source, preprint — CHI 2027 submission, not yet accepted)

TouchTwin’s automatic drafts of material properties scored a middling 4.0/7 for realism. After a median 6.6 minutes of haptic-guided correction, realism rose to 5.6/7 and accuracy against ground truth jumped from 52% to 92%.

It’s a measured case for building a correction affordance into a tangible/haptic interface rather than trusting the model’s first guess.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s dossier re-examined Superhuman Go’s own blog post on agent tool-routing — the lead item in that day’s digest — against the preprint it’s based on, and found the blog systematically misreports its own source: every figure printed as a percentage is actually a percentage-point delta, its stated 180-tool capability threshold is the reverse of what the paper’s own appendix measures, and its “80% of queries get a correct tool” headline is a synthetic-query figure the paper itself reports as 60% on real production traffic.

The preprint’s one genuine new contribution — a precision/recall decomposition against catalog size — holds up under a prior-art check; most of the rest is replication the blog presents as novel. What the paper actually hands the interface is a stable roughly-1-in-5 routing-failure rate that the authors say needs catalog-level fixes or multi-turn clarification — and Superhuman Go’s own help centre documents no agent selector, no direct addressing, and no undo for a misroute, despite marketing copy claiming it “knows which agent is right for the job.”
