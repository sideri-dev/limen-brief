Letting users write their own permission policies for AI agents blocks less overreach than either human-in-the-loop approval or full automation — not more.

A 113-person controlled study put non-technical participants through an 18-action simulated day with 7 built-in overreach actions, under three conditions: approve-every-action, fully automated, or set-your-own-policy-once. The policy condition looked efficient on paper — runtime prompts dropped from 18.0 to 10.9 — but once you count the time spent writing the rules, it saved nothing, and it let through meaningfully more overreach than either alternative (a 20-point gap versus human-in-the-loop).

Tellingly, when people did write policies, 114 of 140 rules were still set to "ask me," not "just do it" — the instinct to delegate real authority is weaker than the industry's "set it and forget it" framing assumes.

An LLM can generate a pedestrian-facing vehicle signal that people understand correctly and still act on incorrectly.

Researchers built a physical eHMI prototype — the light/signal a car uses to communicate with people crossing the street — and ran a 26-participant field study comparing human-designed signals against ones generated by GPT-o3-mini. Comprehension, tested by survey, came out roughly equivalent. Behavior didn't: the LLM-generated signals didn't reliably produce the correct pedestrian reaction on the street, even when people said afterward that they'd understood them.

For any physical or ambient interface meant to trigger a specific safety-relevant action, that's the whole finding — a comprehension survey is not a proxy for behavioral validation.

Watching how a student is behaving, not what they're typing, is what actually cuts unproductive AI-tutor sessions — at real scale.

Four deployments across two intro Python courses (480 students, roughly 180,000 telemetry events) tested prompts triggered by behavioral context — idle time, repeated failed runs, help-avoidance patterns — against prompts triggered by query content alone. Behavior-aware triggering cut the share of "help requested with no independent work attempted first" from 50% to under 21%, and the underlying model held up well out of sample.

This names the actual signal worth building for mixed-initiative interruption in any assistant that has to decide when to jump in uninvited: not what the user typed, but what they were doing beforehand.

A calibration-free ring closes the one gap voice assistants can't: telling which device you mean when you're pointing at it.

PointRing pairs UWB and IMU sensing in a wearable ring to let someone point at a device and have the right one respond, with no per-room calibration step. Across lab and real-home testing on six devices it hit 94.86% selection accuracy, with full deployment taking under three minutes and adding a new device taking under 20 seconds — validated with 43 participants.

The relevant design fact isn't the ring itself, it's the category: voice alone can't disambiguate "which lamp" in a room with three lamps, and this is a concrete number for what a spatial pointing layer costs to add on top of voice.

In a controlled hiring-pipeline study, a better model didn't stop AI-assisted resume fabrication — a human checkpoint in the right place did.

Across 180 runs of an AI resume-improvement pipeline, baseline fabrication sat at 96.7%; adding prompt guardrails cut fabrication density but still left half of all outputs with at least one fabricated claim. What actually worked was placing a human review checkpoint after the resume-improvement step: it eliminated fabricated identity claims outright and cut item-level fabrication to 75%.

A supplementary run swapping in a newer model still produced 90% baseline fabrication — confirming this is a pipeline-design fact, not a model-quality one.
