1. A GUI-agent framework tags every action as reversible, compensatable, or irreversible, treating the task itself as a contract that can be renegotiated mid-flight rather than a fixed plan.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, v1, cs.HC)

Today’s plan-centric agent interfaces leave users watching a procedure while what matters is the state changes underneath it. TaskVM instead classifies every action by how hard it is to undo, and treats the task as an editable contract the user can renegotiate instead of a plan fixed at launch. Holding execution outcomes constant across conditions, the authors measured a real gain in contract compliance and in users’ sense of recovery control and trust — exactly what today’s “approve the plan, then watch” agent UIs are missing.

A reusable taxonomy worth checking against any undo-boundary or approval-gate spec.

2. A new usability metric finds 54% of LLM agent replies fail the person who delegated the task — and a rewrite step nearly halves that rate.

(single-source — 480 replies, eight models, seven families)

The study built a reader-model check for whether a delegated reply is actually usable by the person who asked for it, not whether it’s factually correct — and found the majority fail, with even the best model still failing 30% of the time. Stating explicit requirements for the reply drops the failure rate from 52% to 43%; adding a second model that rewrites the reply drops it to 36%, confirmed by blind human judges.

The finding separates “correct” from “usable” as two different design targets for agent output, with two concrete, measured fixes for the gap.

3. Feedback that only nudges one way — toward more or less reliance on AI advice — quietly narrows a person’s in-the-moment judgment even when team performance looks unchanged.

(single-source — N=244 behavioral study)

Researchers tested feedback interventions aimed at the moment someone overrides AI advice. One-directional feedback produced opposing shifts in reliance that cancel out at the team-performance level while still measurably narrowing reflection-in-the-moment for the individual making the call.

Balanced feedback, able to point either way, didn’t carry that risk — worth checking before a flat performance metric hides the same damage in your own product.

4. Anthropic’s new policy for outside safety evaluators grants deep internal access but reserves redaction rights, not a veto, over what they publish.

(vendor-primary)

The company has onboarded a six-person METR team with its own hardware, office space and internal-tool access, with other METR staff walled off from that team’s work, and names SecureBio and Accenture as active evaluators, Hugging Face as early discussion. Anthropic says it can’t block an evaluator’s publication outright, only require redaction for information inside narrow, predefined categories, under a three-tier access framework still being built out.

5. A simple three-in-a-row launch rule for streaming voice-agent tool calls cuts stale executions from 50% to 3.15%.

(single-source — code published)

RASSG only fires a tool call once three consecutive speech-recognition results arrive with identical, nonempty arguments, with a cancellation policy for calls triggered on input the user has since corrected. Against the LocalAgreement-2 baseline this drops the stale-execution rate from 50.00% to 3.15%, at a mean delay of 3.68 seconds — a small latency cost against a large correctness gain.

A directly reusable default for any duplex voice agent deciding when to act on a still-forming transcript, with working code behind it.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s dossier re-investigated the CHI 2026 confirmation-placement paper that led the previous digest and found the digest’s own framing wrong: the model’s “adaptive, not a fixed interval” claim only holds in simulation, while the condition the 48 participants actually experienced applied one fixed parameter set per domain — making the tested version an interval after all. The measured time gain is concentrated almost entirely in early errors (~29%) and nearly vanishes for mid- and late-task errors (~2% and ~4.5%), and the study never measured error-catching itself, only elapsed time and a single preference question.

The paper is in fact published at CHI 2026 with four independent deposits and eight citing preprints, so the digest’s “not yet in proceedings” single-source marker was also wrong. Against it, a separate live-agent study (N=48) found per-action confirmation produced the highest rate of problematic actions despite scoring best on workload and trust, and no shipped product exposes confirmation frequency as a review checkpoint — most substitute a second model as reviewer instead.
