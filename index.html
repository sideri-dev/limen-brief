1. A field RCT of 9,586 users shows a warmer AI persona isn’t a nice-to-have — it’s an engagement lever whose effect depends on why the person showed up.

A randomized field experiment on a live commercial LLM platform held the model constant and varied only the persona, finding a relational, warmer tone raised sessions 8.1%, session duration 10.6%, and chat rounds 24.2% overall. The effect fractures by declared intent: users there to socialize returned 49.4% more often, task-execution users showed no first-session bump but 12.2% more return visits, and knowledge-exploration users produced more output without more back-and-forth.

The authors’ own framing is the useful part: persona is an operational lever, and interactions and outputs need to be evaluated separately — which argues against shipping one blanket assistant tone and for segmenting persona by what the user is declaring they’re there to do.

2. AR-glasses assistants should watch your hands continuously, not wait for you to ask.

A peer-reviewed IMWUT’26 system, Pro²Assist, tracks task progress continuously through egocentric motion sensing rather than the reactive, query-triggered pattern most assistants default to. Against that reactive baseline it improved procedural-action-understanding accuracy by over 21% and hit up to 2.29x the proactive-timing accuracy, with 90% of a 20-person study finding it useful.

For anyone building an AR or glasses assistant, this is a quantified argument to design the default trigger as continuous step-awareness rather than a wake-word or query box.

3. More autonomy in a near-body robot doesn’t make people trust it more — legible, spatially predictable behavior does.

A CHI’26 Wizard-of-Oz study of wearable supernumerary robotic limbs (N=18) found that increasing the robot’s autonomy did not improve perceived safety or trust on its own; what moved trust was whether the robot’s behavior was spatially calibrated and legible, zone by zone.

That kills “just give it more autonomy” as a default trust fix for any actuator operating close to the body, and points instead to designing explicit, readable proxemic zones.

4. Making someone write one sentence justifying why they accepted an AI suggestion cuts uncritical acceptance of bad ones nearly in half.

In a randomized experiment with 129 students writing data-analysis proposals, requiring a written justification before accepting an AI writing suggestion dropped uncritical adoption of flawed suggestions from 65% to 41% — a 24-point swing — while acceptance of genuinely sound suggestions barely moved (81% vs. 86%).

It’s a cheap, concrete interface pattern: a one-line “why” field before accept/reject, which improves discrimination between good and bad AI output without suppressing the good output.

5. Claude Opus 5.5 cuts pricing up to 60%, sells a 2.5x-faster streaming mode, and permanently locks its “thinking” on — breaking any interface that relied on switching it off for a hard latency budget.

Anthropic’s own docs confirm Opus 5.5 at $4/$20 per million input/output tokens against Opus 5’s $5/$25, with cache reads cut from 10% to 5% of input price. Four breaking changes accompany it: thinking can no longer be disabled, forced tool use now errors, and effort is the new dial (default “medium”).

A separate, waitlisted “Fast mode” sells up to 2.5x output tokens per second for teams whose bottleneck is streaming speed rather than time-to-first-token — a genuinely purchasable interface knob for coding and agent UIs — but any product that depended on disabling thinking to hit a deterministic latency budget at Opus quality now has to redesign around effort or drop to Sonnet 5.

The paper’s own body doesn’t support the headline numbers: it collected 1,256 incidents and analyzed 214, and 83%/74% each print as three different, mutually inconsistent figures across the paper’s own tables. The upstream CHI ‘26 paper supplying the underlying preference data found the real worker-developer gap concentrated on politeness, strictness and imagination — not speed — and reported zero significant misalignment on the very traits the digest’s headline emphasized; the significance threshold used was transplanted from an aggregate-level test to per-task use without the adjustment that made it valid. What survives: a genuinely strong three-stage LLM-validation method, and a defensible read that people-facing systems in the English-language incident record often decide without explanation — with no Japanese, Thai or Korean replication of any of it.
