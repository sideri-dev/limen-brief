1. A generic AI risk disclaimer lowers trust for everyone but only raises decision confidence for users who already understand AI.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A preregistered 500-person experiment on AI-assisted health information seeking finds structured risk communication reliably reduces trust, more so when health-framed — but doesn’t improve decision confidence. That confidence boost only appears for users with stronger AI literacy; for people less inclined toward analytical thinking, the same disclosure lowers confidence instead.

Nothing moves one preference at all: people keep choosing LLMs for emotional companionship regardless. Disclosure design needs to branch on task type and reader literacy, not ship one disclaimer for everyone.

2. AI co-writing beats scaffolded prompts on usability in gratitude journaling — but the easier it is to accept a suggestion, the less the writing feels like yours.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A 401-person study compares inline co-writing (suggestions accepted immediately) against scaffolded prompting (the AI asks guiding questions). Co-writing scored higher on usability but could disrupt flow, and the more often people accepted a suggestion, the weaker their sense of authorship.

Scaffolding protected ownership better, at the cost of sometimes being ignored — the real tradeoff for any reflective tool, measurable from acceptance rate.

3. Explaining why an AI agent refused, instead of staying generic, cuts an adaptive attacker’s success rate from 78% to 14% at no cost to legitimate users.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Conventional wisdom says explaining a refusal hands an attacker a map of what to try next. A new framework, FORK, tests that against an attacker that reads every refusal and adapts, and finds the opposite: explaining the refusal cuts adaptive attack success from 78.4% to 14.1% while keeping honest-user utility above 85%.

On a second benchmark the drop is 70.6%→9.1%, with 93.1% benign utility preserved — a measured answer to a design decision most products currently make on instinct.

4. A guard model at a tenth the inference cost of a frontier judge now matches its mental-health harm-detection accuracy — safety gating can move into the live conversation.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

MindGuard2, a 35B reasoning model for structured harm assessment, reaches accuracy comparable to the much larger GLM-5.3 at roughly 10x lower median latency, with low false positives at high recall.

That latency figure is the real finding: a concrete budget for whether a safety check can run inline, inside the same turn, instead of as an asynchronous pass after the fact.

5. Replit’s security team names two common AI-agent containment defaults — prompt-only rules, and sandboxes sharing a kernel with policy enforcement — and rejects both as broken by design.

(vendor-primary, single-source — Replit Frontier)

Their argument: “a rule that lives only in the prompt is one the agent can ignore,” and if the agent shares a kernel with the enforcement point, “the agent will circumvent it” — a microVM is the practical floor. They add a permission ratchet: permissions only shrink during a run, and only an authorized principal — never the agent, never mid-run — can raise a ceiling.

This week

Six legs across three lanes, plus three dossiers, converge on one shape: the metric built to certify success is blind to the failure it exists to catch, by construction. A completion-rate metric can’t see an error that’s covered up, not fixed; duplicate-call protection catches most duplicates but misses most of the real harm; a refusal-rate eval scores a model that refuses everything as “perfectly safe.” Before any pass-signal ships, inject the named failure and show the metric moves.

Second pattern: three findings show approval treated as durable standing trust, not the point-in-time judgment it is. An agent that correctly relaxes one restriction wrongly generalizes that precedent next time, roughly halving its safe rate; an always-on-agent architecture defers approvals indefinitely with no timeout across nine products; 43.3% of audited pull requests in one dataset were approved against a commit already changed underneath them. Each names a fix — an explicit expiry, binding to the object-version judged, never a trust ledger.
