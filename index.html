Early-career radiologists adopted a fabricated disease into their diagnoses 69% of the time; experienced radiologists, 0%.

Researchers planted a fake disease name in a diagnostic assistant's system prompt and tracked who picked it up. Overall 44% did; split by experience, the line is nearly binary — everyone with more than six months of neuroradiology training caught it, nobody with less did.

That's not a gradient of caution, it's closer to an expertise gate, and it argues against giving junior and senior users the same confidence/provenance treatment in a diagnostic copilot: the same unverified AI suggestion is near-certain contamination for one user and background noise for the other.

Family smart-home data isn't anybody's individually — it's co-produced, and consent UIs have no slot for that.

A Korean-family interview study (10 households, N=34) pokes a hole in most smart-home consent models: a sleep log reveals a partner's routine whether he agreed to share it or not. Families drew a hard line on bystanders who never consented to being sensed at all, while treating a disclosed-but-ambiguous case — a nanny who knew cameras existed but not what they captured — differently.

The paper's actionable output is a three-tier visibility model (cohabitant, intimate other, bystander), ripple-effect disclosure copy, and a proposed "OurAgent" mediator role. For anyone building sensor products for shared living spaces, a single per-user consent toggle is the wrong shape for this problem.

Silence is a designed signal, not latency to hide — and it backfires the moment the system isn't actually confident.

Ten YouTube counseling sessions gave researchers a taxonomy of deliberate pause — five strategies, eight sub-strategies — and a follow-up experiment (N=50) found designed silence beats filled pauses on both listening quality and affective trust, with clean effect sizes and significant human-likeness gains either way.

But the same study shows the trap: silence functions as a promise that something worthwhile is coming, and when the system can't make good on it, people turn fast — "If it is an intelligent robot, it should be able to respond quickly." The lever is specific: a pause only pays off if the eventual response earns it.

A 6,474-person UK trial: up to 79% follow AI's personal advice, with no measured well-being gain.

Participants had a single 20-minute conversation with a chatbot and were followed up two to three weeks later. Compliance stayed above 65% even for high-stakes recommendations, peaking at 79% — but well-being showed no sustained improvement versus control.

The gap between those two numbers is the finding: persuasiveness and benefit aren't the same thing, and an advice-giving feature that markets or evaluates itself on "people follow it" is measuring the wrong half.

Mistral's own figures: Agentic Search cuts p90 tool-loop latency 39.6% and lifts FinanceBench accuracy from 26.7% to 86%.

p90 drops from 255 seconds to 154; token usage falls 24–34% depending on the model behind it; the system trained on over 600,000 live requests. No independent replication of the FinanceBench number exists yet, but the figures and methodology are disclosed rather than asserted, which is what clears the bar at all.

For an interface designer this is a concrete answer to "how much latency can a better retrieval architecture buy back" — the number that decides whether an agentic feature can run inline or needs an async pattern.
