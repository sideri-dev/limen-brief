1. A tool's search result returning a bare count instead of a list is enough to make an agent write to the wrong account — in about a quarter of conversations.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A new study varies whether a tool’s lookup lists, counts, or simply omits a second matching record when one exists, grounding the design in presupposition theory from linguistics. Current frontier models (the newest Claude and GPT models) correctly pause when a second record is merely counted — they open it and check before acting — but a weaker lookup that doesn’t surface the ambiguity produces wrong-account writes in roughly a quarter of conversations.

This locates the failure in the tool’s return shape, not in model capability, which means it’s fixable this week without waiting on a bigger model: a lookup must surface competing records explicitly, not just acknowledge there’s more than one.

2. Geoffrey Litt — now a Design Engineer at Notion, not at Ink & Switch as his piece is sometimes credited — argues raw-diff review and passive explanation-reading both fail, and ships three built things instead.

(single-source — personal blog)

He rejects diff review (“a pile of files edited in alphabetical order with no explanation”) and rejects reading an AI’s own explanation of its work (“books don’t work” — too easy to believe you understood when you didn’t). His fix: an /explain-diff skill, a quiz layer that acts as “a speed regulator” against skimming, and interactive micro-worlds — a Prolog debugger, an agent-built “command center” for a website port. He discloses his own vendor bias, and some of this is built around Notion’s own output format.

The distinction still holds generally: “the agent verified its own work” and “the human can still participate” are different review-UI targets, and a diff, a chat explanation, and a built interactive check are not interchangeable answers to which one you’re solving for.

3. Superfluid, a new open-source local LLM server, holds first-token latency at 1.5 seconds under concurrent multi-agent load where the standard options take 5–10 minutes.

(single-source — open-source artifact)

On a single Apple M5 Pro with eight concurrent agents, Superfluid returns a first token in 1.5s against 291–612 seconds on llama-server, Ollama, and mlx_lm.server. Scaled to six machines across three chip vendors serving 64 users for an hour — machines joining, one killed mid-run — throughput grows from 112 to 534 tok/s and median first-token time falls from 99 to 12s; on twelve Mac Studios it sustains 10,656 tok/s across 38,563 requests from 384 agents without a failure.

Apache-2.0, shipped, not a benchmark claim. The practical point: request-queuing under load, not model size, gates whether “local-first, multi-agent” is a real interface promise or just a demo.

4. A standard memory-permission preview — one allowed example, one blocked example — cannot tell a user which rule actually governs their data, even in the best case.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A combinatorial analysis of every rule consistent with a one-allowed/one-blocked preview finds 36 of 39 possible pairs contain a blocked case no rule in that pair actually allows, and no pair uniquely identifies the rule — the ambiguity is structural, not a copywriting problem. A small pilot backs this practically: of 20 people shown a weak preview of a rule they’d just chosen, half mispredicted or were unsure about at least one of its two uses.

The paper is honest the fix — adaptive, multi-query confirmation, three or four well-chosen tests — is untested for whether it helps users, but the case against the common one-example pattern stands on its own.

5. Notion’s design head is on record rejecting the instinct to surface every new AI feature prominently.

(single-source — press interview)

Randy Hunt: “You can’t have every new tool screaming at you.” He frames Notion’s approach as “tending a garden” — replanting continuously rather than redesigning around each release — and accepts that this costs some immediate feature discovery.

It’s a named, on-record counter-case to “put every AI feature in the primary UI,” and it bears on the disappearing-chat-box question this research has been tracking since Notion’s 2024 UI shift — this time it’s the stated philosophy behind it, not just the shipped surface.
