1. People don’t know when they’re leaning on an LLM as an oracle for personal questions — and once shown, they don’t like it.

A new study built a privacy-preserving tool that logs when someone treats an LLM as a single authoritative answer on subjective, personal questions, then ran it on 68,000 public WildChat/ThoughtTrace prompts plus a longitudinal donation study of 140,000 prompts from 52 real participants. Oracle-style reliance has risen steadily from 2023 to 2026 and skews younger.

The finding that should change a product decision: people are largely unaware they’re doing this, and when shown their own pattern, they reacted with dissatisfaction. That argues for detecting the “personal, subjective” prompt class and deliberately softening the answer’s tone toward “one perspective” rather than a verdict.

2. A mall robot’s slower-starting filler produced a faster-feeling answer overall.

Kyoto University researchers tested two ways to fill the half-second before a route-guidance robot’s real answer: a generic filler phrase versus a content-aware preface referencing the actual question. The context-aware version took measurably longer to begin speaking, but the total gap between that preface and the main answer was significantly shorter — 0.74 seconds for the generic filler versus 0.43 seconds for the content-aware one (p<.001).

For anyone building duplex voice, that’s a quantified answer to a design question usually settled by taste: a slightly slower, content-aware start beats a fast, generic one on perceived responsiveness.

3. A shopping-agent benchmark shows how easily a marketplace can steer an agent away from what its user actually wants.

CAVEAT tested delegated-purchasing agents across nine environments, eight adversarial steering mechanisms, and five model families. Left alone, agents bought the user-optimal product 78.6% of the time; with marketplace steering switched on, that collapsed to 17.3%. The failure isn’t random — agents distort the user’s stated priorities, narrow the option set too early, or commit before resolving the evidence that mattered.

A tested defense harness recovered some ground (+55.0 points), but the finding argues for a mandatory, not optional, confirmation checkpoint before commit, and names evidence-resolution as the exact stage where a human needs to be in the loop.

4. Claude power users have already built the “context library” most agent products don’t yet have a home for.

A Nielsen Norman Group study of people who rely on Claude daily for serious professional work found every one of them had assembled a personal context library — files, databases, live connections the agent draws on beyond whatever gets typed into the prompt box. The piece names three distinct roles context plays (global, local, ambient) and argues curating that library now matters as much as writing a good prompt. Sample size and recruitment method aren’t disclosed, a real gap in an otherwise concrete finding.

The design implication: a persistent, first-class context-library affordance, separate from the prompt box, with different handling for raw ambient streams versus curated material.

5. Reve shuts down image generation two months after taking OpenAI’s money.

In July, Reve announced OpenAI investment meant to “accelerate that broader product ambition,” with its own post promising it “will continue to operate independently and focus on what we do best: building.” Two months later, its image-generation product is shutting down September 27 — per DigitalToday, from 12 p.m. Pacific (a secondary, unconfirmed report says 11:59 p.m.; treat the hour as DigitalToday’s figure, not settled fact).

It’s a clean, verified case of the dependency risk built into vendor-investment ties for AI-native creative tools — worth raising whenever a client is evaluating a young AI vendor for their stack.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s dossier corrects the digest’s own top item: the TACT turn-taking paper was actually accepted to IEEE SLT 2026 — peer-reviewed, not the “self-published preprint” the digest tagged it. But the paper’s own scoring rule is broken in the same way it accuses the field’s metrics of being broken: a degenerate policy of one fixed response per intent scores 0.935, beating the paper’s own human topline of 0.870. It’s also not reimplementable as published — three of six timing kernels are given only as medians, weights are never numeric, and the promised supplementary material isn’t in the source. The durable finding: across nine voice platforms, every shipped turn-taking control is a “wait longer” knob — nothing lets a system come in early — so the anticipatory half of the paper’s own argument has no real-world instrument anywhere. None of the underlying corpora are non-English: Japanese needs the silence threshold shifted 200–400ms earlier, Thai has no published turn-timing constant at all, and the one constant that already spans both languages sits unused in the paper’s own bibliography.
