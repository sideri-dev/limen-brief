1. A decision-theoretic model finally gives agentic UI a real answer to “where does the approval gate go” — and it cuts task time 13.54% while winning 81% preference over confirming only at the end.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — CHI 2026, venue-checked fresh)

Researchers started from an 8-participant formative study naming a recurring error pattern — confirm, diagnose, correct, redo — and modeled confirmation placement as a scheduling problem rather than a fixed interval. In a 48-person test, the adaptive placement beat confirm-at-the-end on both measures: preferred 81% of the time, and faster.

The transferable claim: checkpoint placement in a multi-step agent can be computed from observed failure patterns, not guessed at once per product.

2. A field study of a public sensing robot found that a one-time disclosure notice is worse for trust than no notice at all.

(single-source — HRI ‘26 Companion Proceedings, mutual-review track, lighter than main-track)

Thirty-six people encountered a data-collecting robot under three conditions: no disclosure, a static one-time notice, and dynamic, granular consent. Dynamic consent raised both trust and awareness, as expected. What didn’t survive contact with reality: a static sign discharging the obligation — people trusted the robot less after a one-time notice than after no notice at all.

For anyone designing always-on public sensing, the safe-looking default — post a sign — is not safe; the finding argues for a persistent, interactive consent surface instead. Note this ran in HRI’s Companion track, where review is mutual among submitters, not full double-blind — slightly less weight than a main-track paper if carried further.

3. Mixed-reality notifications should be placed by urgency, not by habit — and getting it right costs nothing in missed alerts.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — TVCG/IEEE VR 2026)

An 18-person study compared a fixed-central notification position against one that moved — periphery, hand, or progressive-center — by urgency. The adaptive version measurably cut mental workload, temporal pressure, and frustration, with alerts noticed just as reliably as the fixed position.

The implication for XR interfaces: a single default notification slot costs users workload with no safety benefit from keeping everything centered.

4. A five-session CHI study pins down exactly when the “novelty wears off” trust dip happens, and what changes it back.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — CHI 2026)

Twelve professional UX evaluators worked with the same AI tool across five sessions, six hours each. Their ignore rate rose sharply from session 1 (8.0%) through sessions 2–4 (16.2%, 13.3%, 17.8%), then recovered — not because trust quietly rebuilt, but because evaluators visibly changed workflow strategy partway through.

“Trust recovers with use” is too vague to design against; what’s worth instrumenting is the workflow-strategy shift itself, not just a trust score.

5. Superhuman’s own account of its internal security agent makes a clean, first-party case against using an AI to check an AI’s work.

(single-source, vendor-primary)

The team built “Barney” on the explicit assumption that it will hallucinate, so verifying its own claims runs through a separate, deterministic, non-AI Python script — “we explicitly chose not to use AI for this, given the risk of reintroducing hallucinations.” Barney’s ability to modify its own instructions is gated by a code-reviewer agent, a stop hook, and human review, with a flat rule against learning any skill that touches real people’s actions.

Useful citable precedent against LLM-judges-LLM verification loops, and a concrete example of a named gate specifically for an agent’s self-modification path.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s dossier tested Linear’s own account of its agent-automation design against the evidence and found it holds up unevenly. The escalation ladder it describes is real but lives only in prompt text — Linear’s own docs expose capability scopes with no pre-execution gate, and the recommended “control” is a sentence in a system prompt, not a guardrail. The “consequences first” ordering matches 18 months of vendor and regulatory consensus (OpenAI, Apple, OWASP, the EU AI Act) but has zero controlling measurement behind it, and the few field measurements of widening agent autonomy over time show scrutiny falling as authority rises, not holding steady.

Letting the agent rewrite its own instructions is already shipped elsewhere, but the one direct controlled comparison found machine-written standing instructions performing worse in five of eight settings at higher cost — a second study flips the sign on different models with the identical change, so the real variable is how the rewrite gets produced, not whether self-editing works. Peer visibility is not a substitute for an approval gate: the only study manipulating shared responsibility for watching an automation found less checking and a quarter fewer failures caught, with no team-level gain.
