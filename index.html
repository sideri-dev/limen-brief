1. Anthropic tried five ways to give an agent memory, and names exactly how each one failed before landing on its current design.

(Anthropic Research, vendor-primary)

Writing to memory indiscriminately (Sonnet 3.7, in a Pokémon-playing test) produced a directory with “413 files and 977,930 characters” — ten times Fable 5’s equivalent — because the game state kept changing and the record kept going stale, costing the agent more time combing notes than playing. A stricter rule, write-only-on-failure, cost both Opus 5 and Opus 4.7 “much of the performance boost that memory gave them.” A shared summary table lost facts outright (41 of 57 survived, versus all 57 when each agent kept its own page), and an unbounded wiki grew to 1.77 times the material it compressed while “effectively abstracting nothing.”

What worked: wiki paired with search beat either alone, and Claude Code caps what auto-loads to the first 200 lines or 25KB. A vendor’s own postmortem on its own system, not a benchmark claim — the first first-party answer to a question this month’s memory-tool coverage has only approached from outside.

2. Anthropic also explains why it stopped trusting single-score safety graders and scripted test users.

(Anthropic Research, vendor-primary)

A model that refuses every self-harm-adjacent prompt “would get a perfect score on a refusal-rate eval” without being any safer — so Anthropic moved graders to sets of yes/no questions instead of one overall number. Scripted adversarial test scripts “can’t anticipate” how a model will actually respond, so a separate Claude model now generates the user’s side of the conversation instead.

The same page discloses that Anthropic “temporarily stopped running many evaluations that give models internet access” and added real-time monitoring for the ones that still do. This is a usable methodology for anyone designing human-in-the-loop review or red-teaming, not just Anthropic’s own process.

3. An EEG study on AI tutoring finds that the design judged “best” by test score is the one that gives answers away.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, MIT Media Lab)

MIT Media Lab researchers put 50 participants through three chatbot-tutor conditions wearing a Muse EEG headband. The unrestricted, no-guardrails chatbot produced the highest immediate post-test scores (p < .03, d > 0.80), while the EEG-adaptive, Socratic-style condition produced significantly higher engagement on the brain data (p = .018). The authors’ own reading: the unrestricted chatbot’s win “is not an evidence of deeper learning, but rather a result of the immediate post-test evaluation” — the measurement window rewarded the shallowest design.

Double-blind, ACM-archived full-paper track at HAI 2026 (Osaka, Nov 16-19). The lesson travels past tutoring: an evaluation window is itself a design decision. Research: Alexandre Clin Deffarges, Nataliya Kosmyna, Pattie Maes, arXiv:2609.00584.

4. Teachers in Punjab drew a sharp, standard-shaped line around what they’d let AI touch.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Nine of thirteen teachers studied took AI help with lesson planning, but none accepted AI-drafted learning objectives, and most wouldn’t let AI handle emotional support for students. Several skipped AI grading even where answer keys existed, because students expect evidence of a teacher’s own effort, not just a correct mark.

The researchers’ proposed fix: have the tool ask which external standard applies before generating, show the output beside that standard, and budget the checking time as a real cost — a standard is necessary but not sufficient for delegation. Research: arXiv:2611.27533.

5. Five years of interviews with Chinese digital painters show the AI-collaboration boundary moving, not fixed.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Seventeen painters interviewed annually from 2021 to 2025 moved from blanket resistance — protecting observation, originality, signature, ownership — to bounded delegation: AI handling references, backgrounds, rough sketches, client-facing drafts, while copyright and creative labor stayed off-limits throughout. The shift tracked task type and career stage, not just model generation.

The implication: a revisable boundary control with provenance scaffolding and community authorship norms, not a single static permission setting. Research: arXiv:2609.09333.
