1. Superhuman Go’s tool-router loses recall twice as fast as precision at scale — and the fix that worked was embedding, not wording.

At production scale, Superhuman’s agent platform saw its flat LLM tool-router’s F1 drop 16-23% scaling from 10 to 110 agents, driven mostly by recall collapsing from 55% to 37%. The post ranks what the team tried and rejected first: richer tool descriptions did almost nothing, lexical search did worse than no fix at all (enterprise tool vocabularies overlap too much), hierarchical LLM routing underperformed everything, and platform-native tool search plateaus past about 180 tools.

What won was embedding-based shortlisting before the router acts, beating flat routing at every scale point by 6-11%. For a multi-tool or multi-agent harness, this names the real failure mode behind “just add more tools” and gives a falsified checklist to test against.

2. Wearable users score higher on obsessive-compulsive traits, and a field-wide census finds almost nobody is checking for it.

(single-source, peer-reviewed — N=264 survey plus a 782-paper literature review)

A US survey found wearable users scored significantly higher on obsessive-compulsive traits than non-users, with roughly one in four reporting a negative psychological impact from calorie tracking, smart notifications, or sleep tracking. The same paper reviewed 782 papers across five flagship wearable and mobile-computing venues and found only 5 of 123 wearable-focused papers addressed negative psychological impacts at all.

That count is itself the citable argument: psychological-impact testing belongs on the standard pre-ship checklist for any health or fitness AI feature, and right now it almost never happens.

3. In a hospital study, explaining an AI forecast’s uncertainty did nothing — and just stating the uncertainty got misread.

(single-source — N=16 on-site evaluation)

Sixteen hospital staff (ward managers and nurses) evaluated an AI forecasting tool on-site; the uncertainty it communicated was frequently misread and lowered decision confidence, while explaining that uncertainty moved neither confidence nor advice acceptance.

Product teams often bundle “communicate uncertainty” and “explain it” into one transparency feature; here neither substituted for the other, so each needs its own test.

4. Tuning an explanation against how people actually decide — not how convincing it sounds — moved human accuracy from near-chance to 63.7%.

(single-source — N=693)

Ordinary explanations from an LLM (Qwen 3.6) left human decision accuracy at a near-random 51.3%. Optimizing explanations against a model of actual human decisions, rather than plausibility, raised that to 63.7% — beating other RL baselines by over 8 points.

The real design target for a decision-support explanation surface: tune against human decision outcomes directly, not how reassuring it reads.

5. A trust score after working with an AI doesn’t predict what someone will actually choose to delegate next time.

(single-source — N=56, 392 task observations)

Across 56 participants and 392 task observations, trust and delegation stayed distinct — verification, not delegation, was the dominant choice even at high reported trust. A post-task trust survey can’t stand in for predicting real delegation behavior; a product needs to measure the reject/verify/delegate choice directly.

Yesterday’s dossier

Yesterday’s dossier re-examined the DISARM disclosure interface behind the previous digest’s lead item and found its 78% belief-shift result is an oracle condition, not a shippable design: the “manipulative tactic” tags it displayed were the agent’s own pre-committed plan, and the authors’ own attempt to detect those tactics independently only reached a fraction of human-rater agreement. The disclosure arm’s belief shift actually landed close to where a no-manipulation baseline conversation already sits in the parent dataset, so the design can’t distinguish “restored the user’s judgment” from “suppressed ordinary belief-updating.”

No regulation anywhere requires disclosing an AI’s hidden incentive, and current vendor policy still treats the underlying concealment as compliant. The corrected takeaway: the interface’s measured benefit depends on a detector nobody has built at usable accuracy, not on the disclosure design itself.
