1. Thoughtworks calls out “human-in-the-loop theater” — a human approval step nobody can actually evaluate — and pairs it with a concrete fix.

(Technology Radar Vol. 35)

The latest Technology Radar places the pattern itself in Caution: keeping a human in an agent’s loop as “an empty approval step rather than a real check,” where the human “approves actions, prompts or diffs they can’t realistically evaluate” and becomes “an accountability sink: no one truly owns the decision.” The paired fix, “Risk-tiered human oversight” (Trial), routes by reversibility and damage instead of uniform sign-off: humans apply the riskiest, hardest-to-reverse changes themselves, review intermediate-risk ones, and let low-risk changes auto-merge through CI via codebase-zone flags and a fallible LLM impact classifier.

The entry cites Meta’s own published process (arXiv:2605.30208) as a worked example, not a bare assertion. If you default agent workflows to “someone clicks approve,” this names why that’s theater and what to route by instead.

2. Quantizing a small chat model to 4-bit scrambles its refusal behavior — and the scramble tracks the specific rounding error, not the bit width.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A University of British Columbia preprint finds that with a compliant reply prefilled, a 4-bit Qwen2.5-3B continues into harmful content more often than its full-precision parent, while on held-out jailbreaks a 4-bit Llama-3.2-3B complies 10.3 points less — opposite directions on two models both “just quantized.” The methodology has teeth: shuffled or Gaussian-noise control perturbations move behavior near zero, while real rounding error moves it near-linearly.

The upshot for anyone shipping an on-device chat model: it cannot inherit its parent’s safety eval — the specific quantized artifact has to be re-tested against itself. Research: Ege Kaya, arXiv:2611.22186.

3. Same Radar: stop relaying a coding agent’s build failures through a human, and give the agent its own eyes on the pipeline.

(Technology Radar Vol. 35, Assess ring)

Thoughtworks’ “Agent pipeline responsibility” entry makes the case bluntly: “An agent that can’t see the build status can’t respect a team agreement not to push on red, while a human relaying failure logs into a chat window becomes a meat proxy.”

The fix is mechanical: expose pipeline status and logs through the agent’s own tools so it can see what broke and help fix it, and enforce the rules that matter with pre-push and server-side gates rather than hoping the agent behaves on its own.

4. A home activity-recognition system that only wakes the camera when cheaper sensors say something is actually happening.

(arXiv preprint)

OrganicHAR pairs privacy-preserving ambient sensors — radar, lidar, thermal arrays — with a vision-language model it calls on sparingly: queries to the VLM drop 90%, while accuracy holds at 79% for coarse activity categories and 73% for finer-grained ones once wearables are added, across a 12-person study. It’s new to this corpus despite being roughly a year old by its real publication date (an ACM journal embargo delayed the arXiv posting).

The design decision it hands anyone building in-home sensing with a camera or VLM: gate the expensive, privacy-sensitive sensor behind cheap ambient ones, and only invoke it at flagged moments rather than continuously. Research: Prasoon Patidar et al., arXiv:2605.18455.

5. OpenAI’s newest models drop support for the old tool-calling API outright, and introduce a real price lever for agents that can wait.

(vendor changelog, independently corroborated)

GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol support Chat Completions for ordinary use, but “tool calling requires the Responses API” — full stop, per OpenAI’s own changelog, independently confirmed by Simon Willison. The same changelog entry introduces background-completion windows: a two-hour window prices 30% below standard, an eight-hour window 40% below.

That’s the first OpenAI-priced incentive making an always-on, non-interactive agent run cheaper to simply leave running than to invoke on demand. Anyone with a tool-calling agent still on Chat Completions needs to migrate or lose access to these models’ tool use entirely.

Yesterday’s dossier

A correction of yesterday’s #1 item. The digest said a walking-training robot’s uniformly positive feedback “measurably worsens affect and perceived competence” in older adults — the dossier found perceived competence was flat to three decimals, and the two items that actually moved were “professional” and “leaves things to chance.” Of 43 single-item tests run, five hit p < .05 and none survives any standard multiple-comparison correction; four of the five “significant” results sit right at the arithmetic threshold a sample this size could even detect.

No performance comparison exists on any objective measure, though scores were announced to both groups and walking logs existed. The agent’s own script had no path to agree a session had gone badly — which matches, item for item, what gerontology already calls elderspeak, a literature the paper never cites.
