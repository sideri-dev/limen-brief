1. A 250,000-conversation Stanford study of real Claude.ai usage finds most chats are high-stakes and human-led, with friction worked through rather than abandoned.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A privacy-preserving analysis of 249,834 real consumer conversations (April–May 2026) found 56% reach the “consequential” tier or higher, 72% stay human-led, and the AI teaches the user something in 67%. Friction appears in roughly half of conversations, and in 78.7% of those people work through it rather than quit.

This replaces guesswork with a population-scale baseline: invest in friction-recovery and approval gates where stakes already concentrate, not evenly everywhere.

2. EEG evidence names “the vibe paradox”: agentic coding assistants raise cognitive load on every measured activity, even as trust rises and motivation on your own project falls.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

An 11-person, 22-session crossover (Copilot Chat vs. Cursor Agents) found load higher with the agent running on every activity measured, several differences large and statistically reliable. Trust rose with use while motivation on the user’s own project dropped.

This undercuts the assumption that delegation lowers burden, and shows trust and motivation are separate dials — not one axis a self-report trust score can stand in for.

3. The dominant pattern across nearly 3,000 real chats is that people never bring independent judgment to begin with — it doesn’t erode partway through.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Scoring 2,877 WildChat conversations found population-level autonomy stays flat across a conversation (only 4.7% decline meaningfully) because 80.8% never had much autonomy to lose. Engagement, though, is self-reinforcing once started, roughly doubling turn over turn.

The intervention point moves: decay-correction mid-conversation solves a problem that barely exists; getting a disengaged user to engage once has the real payoff.

4. Warp explains why its coding agent lives in the same list structure as a shell command, not a bolted-on chat panel.

(single-source — vendor blog, April 29, 2026)

The company names three rejected designs — a flat character grid, a plain array, a mutable scrollback buffer whose storage layer “deliberately doesn’t support insert” — replaced by an ordered list of typed blocks where anything that can report its own height sits beside terminal output. The agent isn’t a separate UI mode; it’s the same structural primitive as everything else in the tool.

5. Builders of AI companion apps experience their own agents as making independent choices — even when they wrote the rules themselves.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, thin sample)

Across 12 open-source agentic-companion projects plus 10 builder/user interviews, whether a limit holds depends on whether it’s enforced in code or just requested in a prompt. More strikingly, builders who wrote their own companion’s constraints still experienced it as choosing independently — perceived autonomy shows up wherever a configuration leaves a choice open, including to its own author.

Actionable version: expose which guardrails are code-enforced versus prompt-requested as a visible setting, not a hidden implementation detail. Thin sample, kept for a clean design implication despite modest evidentiary weight.
