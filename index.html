1. A CHI 2026 taxonomy names a design surface most agent interfaces don’t support: quietly guiding a running agent, versus stopping it.

(ACM CHI 2026, Barcelona)

A controlled study (N=30, 12 live-site tasks) splits user interventions on a running web agent into two kinds — “explicit” ones that halt or override an action, and “implicit” ones that guide or pre-stage the environment without stopping execution at all. Today’s agent UIs are mostly pause/stop, an explicit-only vocabulary; this taxonomy argues the implicit kind — correcting a filter mid-run, pre-filling a field — is where most repair actually happens, and it’s invisible to current designs.

The ACM DL page itself 403’d behind Cloudflare, so this was corroborated independently through Crossref’s metadata and the authors’ own page rather than the publisher — both carry the abstract verbatim. One claim got cut rather than published: a submitted “CHI honorable mention” credit couldn’t be confirmed against SIGCHI’s own program site and isn’t asserted here.

2. End-users — the people AI transparency features are supposedly built for — rate them worst of three stakeholder groups surveyed.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Nov 3 2026)

A global survey of 400 end-users, builders and policymakers, plus a focus group, found only 28% of end-users think current AI transparency practices reflect their needs well — against 53% of builders and 49% of policymakers rating the same practices favorably. That’s a concrete argument against treating a builder’s or regulator’s sign-off on a disclosure feature — a model card, an “AI-generated” label, a confidence score — as evidence it actually serves the person it’s nominally for.

The paper argues for plural, situational transparency infrastructure rather than one universal design; it’s two days old and single-source, but every headline figure checked out verbatim across four places in the paper body.

3. Treating a robot swarm as a hand-deformable material raises felt control over it — without raising the sense that it’s part of your body.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Nov 4 2026; accepted ACM ISS 2026)

A VR study (N=17) had people control a robot swarm either through a fluid, hand-aperture-driven metaphor or a standard avoidance algorithm; the fluid version succeeded in all 17 trials against 8 of 17 for the baseline, completed tasks roughly twice as fast, and scored far higher on felt agency — but not on felt body-ownership, which stayed flat.

That’s a second independent finding (after a teleoperation study published here November 3) that agency and body-ownership dissociate in embodied remote control: designing for one doesn’t automatically buy the other. One catch: a cited ACM DOI doesn’t actually resolve; the venue claim itself (ACM ISS 2026) checked out independently, so the finding stands, just not that specific DOI.

4. A four-week study of Snapchat’s My AI finds trust is renegotiated continuously, not rated once — and that making it feel more human can backfire over time.

(accepted ACM UMAP ’26, Gothenburg)

Twenty-seven participants tracked across a month showed trust in the companion isn’t a single first-session judgment; conversational fluency keeps people engaged, but “excessive anthropomorphism and limited transparency” erode trust as the relationship continues. That’s a field finding rather than a lab snapshot, and it argues directly against a common default in persona design: dialing up human-likeness to win early engagement isn’t free past the first few sessions.

Independently confirmed via a second source as accepted to UMAP ’26 in Gothenburg — an adjacent, legitimate ACM venue, not a core HCI one, so weighted accordingly.

5. The same model’s rule-compliance rate swings 11.5 points depending purely on which agent harness wraps it.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Nov 4 2026)

Across 3,120 two-turn episodes testing four open-weight models in five different harnesses against paraphrased EU banking-conduct rules, pooled compliance sat around 87–92% — but holding the model constant and changing only the harness moved the result by 11.5 percentage points. A same-model review step cut violations from 9.6% to 6.4%, which the authors flag as contrary to their own pre-registered prediction.

The practical point: evaluating “the model” in isolation for an autonomy decision misses where the risk actually lives — scaffolding design changes how much autonomy an interface can safely grant.
