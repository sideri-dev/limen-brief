1. Production data on 541,621 reviewer decisions shows the obvious proxies for a good human reviewer — activity, speed, agreement, tenure — are actively misleading.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A six-month dataset from an AI-assisted accounting review system, 483 reviewers, found scoring on behavior alone “inverts the ground truth”: an overconfident group scored higher (5.2% of corrections survived scrutiny) than genuine experts (99.2%). Quality didn’t track tenure at all — not a skill picked up on the job. The trait that actually separates good reviewers is whether stated confidence tracks correctness; applying that logic to the AI model concentrated 92.5% of human effort on errors that mattered.

Names the proxy to use for override authority in human-in-the-loop review, and the ones to stop trusting — though under majority voting, one person’s value can’t always be pinned down. Research: Sinha & Pandey, arXiv:2611.24932 (cs.HC).

2. A deliberately imperfect confidence display trained people better than an accurate one did.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

In a preregistered 300-novice study rating a 94%-accurate classifier, only the original, conservative display improved people’s calibration afterward, by toning down overconfidence — a display matching the model’s true (more extreme) confidence taught nothing extra. In the researchers’ words: “the display that taught best was the one miscalibrated for the model.”

That splits two jobs designers usually conflate — trusting the AI right now, versus training independent judgment for later — and the second wants a display deliberately softer than the model’s real confidence. Research: Hegeman, Hasan, Lee, Caplin & Trueblood, arXiv:2611.23618.

3. Linear now runs every pull request through an AI reviewer first, locking a human in only when the change is sensitive.

(single-source — vendor’s own data)

Self-approval after agent review grew from ~2.5% of merges (Jan–Jun) to 53% by October; review requests per author fell from 85 to 54 a month. The one mandatory, hard-coded exception is authentication, infrastructure, or deployment config — and even there the mandatory-review rate drops with tenure (79% in reviewers’ first three months, 46% after two years), suggesting trust calibrates inside the gate too.

A clean worked example of where to draw the human-approval line: agent-first, self-approval as the default exit, a rule (not judgment) for when a second human is non-negotiable. Research: Linear, Engineering Data.

4. Granola sent its AI agent straight to the full meeting transcript every time, and answers got worse, not better.

(single-source — vendor’s own data)

With unrestricted transcript access, the agent skipped search entirely 34% of the time, and started quoting sources it had only paraphrased — up to 40% of answers carried an unsupported “lightly reworded” quote. Transcript-first also meant it missed things its own notes already covered. The fix: notes as the index, transcript as fallback, paraphrase by default — which lifted transcript-search use to 83% and coverage on a 69-question test set from 40% to 79%.

For any product giving an agent two sources of truth, paraphrase-by-default and notes-before-transcript beats a bigger search index. Research: Granola, Josh Tuddenham.

5. A new modeling study shows the standard “trim the agent’s context to cut cost” move can raise your bill instead.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Across 20,000+ coding-agent runs on SWE-bench and related benchmarks, retrieving only relevant code cut read/search commands 18–34% — but with a provider cache making repeat input ten times cheaper, that same trimming raised total cost 33–42%, with no gain in task success. Without a cache discount, trimming saves as expected (19–36%); with one, it can cost up to 116% more.

Shorter prompts aren’t automatically cheaper, because a leaner context can make the agent take more steps. Model cost against your provider’s caching discount and the agent’s resulting behavior, not raw token count. Research: Yang, Dong, Xue et al., arXiv:2611.22238, “Context Has a Price.”
