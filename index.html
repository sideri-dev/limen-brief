1. A 1,524-incident workplace study puts a number on a design instinct: defaulting to speed over precision is what breaks AI tools for the people using them.

An ACM FAccT ‘26 study analyzed 1,524 real workplace AI incidents across 171 occupational tasks and 12 industry sectors, then compared them against stated preferences from 202 workers and 197 developers. 83% of the incidents trace to a mismatch between what workers wanted — systems that are precise, insightful, or personal — and what they got, which skewed basic, simple, or general; 74% of that is attributable to developers overfocusing on efficiency and speed.

A large-N, peer-reviewed number behind an argument design teams usually make on instinct alone: shipping the fast, generic version of a feature is itself the failure mode.

2. Criticize the AI in front of the user, and the user hears it as criticism of themselves — but only when it’s negative.

A CHI 2026 experiment had participants delegate a scheduling task to either a human or an AI assistant, then varied the assistant’s competence and the feedback it received. Negative feedback toward the AI delegate got internalized as self-directed criticism — the paper’s own term is an “AI Phantom Limb” effect — while positive feedback transferred much less, and neither asymmetry showed up when the delegate was human. (One sub-claim, about low-competence assistants triggering more self-blame, couldn’t be confirmed against the paper’s own text and was dropped.)

The finding argues against UI copy that scolds an AI agent in front of the user during error or correction flows — the rebuke lands on the person, not the software — and for keeping system-fault language separate from user-fault language in any repair interface.

3. Anthropic stopped letting Claude grade its own work — not because self-critique is impossible to prompt for, but because it structurally doesn’t work.

In its own engineering writeup, Anthropic describes agents asked to evaluate their own output confidently praising it “even when, to a human observer, the quality is obviously mediocre.” Their fix wasn’t a better self-critique prompt — it was separating the agent that does the work from the agent that judges it, then tuning that second evaluator to be skeptical, which proved far more tractable than making a generator self-critical.

A first-party admission with a named mechanism, and a direct architectural note for anyone building an AI design-review loop: the judge has to sit outside the maker.

4. Gemini 3.8 Live now bills voice by the minute and genuinely lets you interrupt it mid-sentence — but “enterprise-ready” is still private preview, not GA.

Google’s pricing page confirms per-minute audio billing ($0.005/min in, $0.018/min out) alongside the usual per-token rate, and Simon Willison independently verified the interruption behavior over the live WebSocket connection. The model also auto-switches between 97 languages mid-conversation and narrates background tool calls as they run. Worth correcting: the announcement reads like a general release, but Google’s own rollout language puts enterprise access at “private preview,” with only developer access live today.

Per-minute pricing plus real barge-in makes an always-on voice interface something you can actually cost out and design as a continuous channel — the question becomes what the interface does while it’s being interrupted, not when to open the mic.

5. Cocoa treats delegation as something you renegotiate step by step, not a single switch you flip at the start.

A CHI 2026 system lets users assign individual steps of a plan to themselves or an AI agent, and lets the plan keep adjusting as steps complete rather than locking in upfront. A lab study of 16 people found it added steerability without hurting ease of use, and a week-long field deployment with 7 researchers showed the pattern holding on real work.

A concrete alternative to the single global autonomy toggle most agent tools ship — approval and handoff controls belong at the level of individual steps, reassignable as work unfolds, not one decision made upfront.

Yesterday’s dossier

Withholding under the clock substantially corrects yesterday’s #1 item. The WriteAid paper is titled “When Scaffolding Breaks,” was published in CHI 2026 proceedings five months ago (not “conditionally accepted”), and analyzed 133 of 157 students, not 157. Its headline claim — that scaffolding demotivated lower-proficiency students — is an inference from unsignificance-tested counts in a single-arm, no-control design with a mid-study model change, not a measured result. The strongest comparable evidence, a preregistered PNAS study of nearly 1,000 students, found the opposite: answer-withholding won, with no significant heterogeneity by proficiency. What survives: making struggle “visible” only surfaces whatever the tool detects, so the detector inventory is the real design decision.
