1. Figma rejected a flat network allowlist and a global lock for the sandboxes that run AI-written code — “that’s often too blunt for the products we want to build.”

(vendor-primary, single-source — Figma Blog, Nov 4 2026)

Figma’s new “Nimbus” platform centralizes how it isolates AI-generated and agent-written code, after discarding several simpler designs. The allowlist was dropped for method-level restriction instead — trusted hosts get full access, everything else gets GET/HEAD/OPTIONS only, enough for web search while blocking POST/PUT.

Credentials are encrypted and swapped for their real value only at the intended destination, so exfiltrated code gets nothing exploitable; the global claim-lock became a single Postgres transaction per claim. Warm pools cut provisioning latency 90%, availability rose from 99% to 99.95% — a concrete infrastructure-layer answer to a decision every agent-code-running production system has to make.

2. An AI that infers what you already know and leaves it unstated beats one that spells everything out — on speed and detection both.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Nov 2 2026)

A preregistered 114-person study had people monitor four simulated delivery drones for problems, comparing alerts from a “pragmatic” planner that models what the listener already knows against a fully-specified baseline. The pragmatic version caught more real problems (85.8% vs 78.5% detection, p=.0032), with the gain concentrated where awareness was already low and absent where it was already high — no measured cost in mental demand or frustration.

Exhaustive disclosure isn’t a safe default; inferring what’s already understood can win on both speed and accuracy.

3. A head-worn gaze sensor skips the camera entirely — trading eye-tracking accuracy for structural bystander privacy.

(single-source — ACM UIST 2026)

GazeTags are $2.20 infrared tags a head-worn device locates via a narrow infrared beam aligned to head direction, instead of a camera reading eye position. The CMU Future Interfaces Group’s reasoning: cameras cause “discomfort around image capture” and uncertainty about what’s recorded, so dropping image capture removes that problem structurally, not through a policy or an indicator light.

The cost is conceded: head direction is only a proxy for eye gaze, which can deviate well outside it, and the authors “cannot track eye gaze” directly. Bench testing held detection above 99% with up to 5 tags at 4–6 meters, dropping as tag count and distance rose; battery life runs roughly 1.4 years on a coin cell. A sensing-modality tradeoff for any gaze- or context-aware wearable: camera-free buys privacy by design, at a measured accuracy cost to weigh against what the product needs to sense.

4. Adding slider/dropdown controls next to the prompt box raises people’s sense of control over an AI’s output — without changing whether the AI gets the preference right.

(peer-reviewed — ACM UIST 2026)

A 12-person within-subjects study compared “Malleable Prompting” — sliders, dropdowns and toggles with a live comparison view — against natural-language-only prompting for controlling LLM generation. Sense of control, transparency, and cause-effect understanding were all significantly higher with widgets (p=.002, .005, .012), and target-preference quality was modestly higher (p=.036) — but conveying what people wanted showed no significant difference.

Add adjustable controls alongside the text box, not instead of it: the gain is in felt control and comparing iterations, not in the model understanding the preference, which plain language already does about as well.

5. Pasting tool results back into an LLM’s context works fine at small table sizes and collapses almost completely past about 74 rows — at 200x the cost per correct answer.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 30 2026)

A production award-travel agent compared two architectures for handing tabular data to a tool-calling model: re-emitting full records into context every turn (“SYNTHESIS”) versus a typed side channel the model never regenerates (“BYPASS”). Across 339 scored turns and four models, SYNTHESIS scored 45.7% fully correct overall but collapsed to 8% or worse beyond ~74 rows; BYPASS held 92.3% correct flat across every table size and model, at a flat $0.012 versus ~200x that cost per correct SYNTHESIS answer past 100 rows.

The lesson: route records through a deterministic channel rather than letting the model re-emit them — the failure is silent and scales with data size, not conversation length.
