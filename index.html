1. A walking-training robot that praised elderly trainees no matter what made them feel worse — and more likely to say they’d keep training.

(single-source — peer-reviewed journal)

24 older adults did two weekly sessions on a robot with a tablet virtual trainer, comparing uniformly positive feedback against none. The positive condition raised irritation and sadness and made the trainer feel less professional, while also raising stated intent to continue — engagement and felt experience moved opposite ways in the same study.

Worth sitting with before defaulting a coaching agent to upbeat feedback: a rising “I’ll keep using this” number can mask people feeling worse. Research: 吉田直人ほか, ヒューマンインタフェース学会論文誌 28(3):289–300, DOI 10.11184/his.28.3_289.

2. Robot companionship and VR support an elderly person’s emotional engagement on different timelines, not just different strengths, of presence.

(single-source — peer-reviewed journal)

Thirty-four older adults did a three-week supportive-dialogue study split between a physical robot and immersive VR. VR won early — more future-oriented talk, more positive emotion — but the robot caught up and pulled ahead later; both improved how alive the system felt, and VR read as more intelligent throughout.

The design question for a longitudinal supportive system for older users isn’t “robot or VR, which is more present” — it’s which embodiment fits the week you’re actually running. Research: 林勇吾ほか（立命館大学ほか）, ヒューマンインタフェース学会論文誌 28(3):369–384, DOI 10.11184/his.28.3_369.

3. Most “unhealthy reliance” on companion AI isn’t actually harming anyone — a new taxonomy separates the two for the first time with real data.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Coding 110 adults’ real Character.AI and Replika conversations against a 14-behavior taxonomy, researchers found overreliance behaviors in 63% of non-roleplay chats but observable harm in only 10.5% — and open-source classifiers caught most behaviors reliably.

A blanket “you’re relying on this too much” nudge misses most of the time; the case is for graduated, trajectory-based intervention over a flat trigger. Research: Nguyen, Li, Wan, Agarwal, Nguyen, Hwang & De Choudhury, arXiv:2611.25734.

4. The coding-agent memory tool flagged two days ago turns out to have real numbers behind it — and they say exposing memory as a reviewable list, not hiding it, is what works.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, update)

A 132-person study found developers using a tool that surfaces an agent’s memory writes as editable, scopable items did measurably better work and trusted the agent’s memory more, and the gain came from better monitoring of what the agent remembered, not from doing less work. They monitored more and felt no more burdened.

A one-week, nine-developer field deployment followed. The concrete answer to “what should an agent’s memory UI look like”: a list you can watch and edit, not a black box. Research: Zhang, Li, Luo, Tang, Zhang, Piao, Liu, Ma & Ma, “MemoSync,” arXiv:2611.24165.

5. A guardrail bolted outside a mobile agent — not a smarter model — is what gets it from catching 3 in 10 risky actions to catching nearly all of them.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A runtime layer that can allow, block or force a user-approval step, plus a route-caching layer for repeat tasks, raised GPT-4o’s high-risk-action blocking from 29.7% to 97.0%, cut model calls needed by up to 6x, and lifted repeat-use task success to 95.7%.

Deciding how a mobile agent earns authority over payments or private data: the answer isn’t a better-aligned model, it’s a gate built outside it. Research: Jang, Kim, Yu & Kim, “MATE,” arXiv:2611.25526 (comment: accepted to EMNLP 2026).
