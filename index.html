1. Older adults accept home-IoT awareness but not automated authority — and family “protection” erases the distinction anyway.

(peer-reviewed, ACM IMWUT / UbiComp 2026 — interview study, N=22)

A UbiComp 2026 interview study of 22 people — those over 65 and the family members who help manage their always-on home IoT — found a consistent split: people are fine being observed by ambient sensors but want a say before anything acts on what those sensors see. In practice that distinction rarely survives contact with a family caregiver’s good intentions: the paper finds preferences are “frequently bypassed through structural power asymmetries rooted in device acquisition and management pathways, often rationalized by family members’ protective intent.”

For anyone designing a shared-control home system, the fix isn’t a better sensing-disclosure UI — it’s an ownership and administration model that keeps “may observe” and “may act” as separate, renegotiable grants rather than one onboarding toggle.

2. A Japan-Germany driverless-car study finds there’s no single “right” way for a vehicle to signal its intentions to pedestrians — there are two.

(peer-reviewed, ACM IMWUT / UbiComp 2026 — N=40, 20 Germany / 20 Japan)

Researchers ran the same multi-objective optimization — comparing external vehicle displays (eHMI) against each other via Bayesian search — with 20 participants in Germany and 20 in Japan, then compared the resulting Pareto fronts: the sets of designs that no other design beats on every criterion at once. The two fronts diverge — what wins the tradeoff between clarity, trust and unobtrusiveness in Japan isn’t what wins it in Germany.

That’s a direct challenge to any plan for a single global eHMI standard for autonomous vehicles signaling right-of-way, and a concrete argument for region-specific defaults where a product’s market spans both Japan and Europe.

3. A per-person, learned threshold beats every prompt-and-model combination at predicting how far a shopping agent should go before asking permission.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Researchers trained a lightweight model on a few dozen of a single person’s own past purchase-approval labels and used it to predict that person’s “auto-purchase line” — the boundary between what the agent can just buy and what it must confirm first. With only 30-40 labels it hit a median accuracy of 0.79, beating every prompt-engineering and model-choice combination tested — and the boundary it learns doesn’t transfer from one shopping domain to another.

The implication for any agent that acts with money or consequence on a user’s behalf: a single global confirmation policy, however well-prompted, is a worse fit than a per-user, per-domain threshold learned from that user’s own behavior.

4. 2-bit quantization now recovers full reasoning capability, not just raw accuracy, via a new self-distillation method.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, Qwen3 1.7B/4B/8B)

A technique called OnQ (on-policy distillation with candidate-wise Dynamic KL) closes the gap between a heavily compressed model and its full-precision original — at 2 bits per weight, tested on Qwen3 at 1.7B, 4B and 8B parameters, it “outperforms every baseline on every benchmark at every model size,” improving the 8B model’s average score by 10.26 points over the next-best compression method.

That’s a meaningful shift in where the on-device/local-inference boundary sits for reasoning-capable models — a model that previously needed full precision to reason well may now run usably compressed on far more modest hardware.

5. A CMU study of commercial computer-use agents finds users like them — then get stuck because the agents can’t tell you what they don’t know.

(peer-reviewed, ACM CAIS 2026 — 102-agent survey + N=31 usability study)

The researchers surveyed marketing claims from 102 commercial agent products and ran a 31-person usability study against the agents actually built. Users were “generally impressed” at first contact, but ran into a consistent failure mode: “agent capabilities that were misaligned with user mental models” and agents “lacking the meta-cognitive abilities necessary for effective collaboration” — they can’t represent, to themselves or the user, what they can and can’t reliably judge about their own output.

That’s a concrete design target: an agent status or capability-disclosure surface that states its own confidence boundaries, not just its actions, is the gap behind most of today’s agent-marketing overreach.

Yesterday’s dossier

Contests the framing of yesterday’s own #1 item (the CharacterAI companionship study). Read at full text, the paper’s “social displacement” mediation claim rests on a mediator measured only once, at the same wave as the outcome — a design the mediation-methods literature says typically overestimates the indirect effect, with the proper fix needing at least three waves the study doesn’t have.

The citation record compounds it: the same co-author’s own three-year follow-up (loneliness effect exactly 0.00) and a 275,728-participant meta-analysis finding “not loneliness” are both omitted, while two displacement authorities the paper does cite actually conclude there’s “very little direct or causal evidence” for the mechanism. An independent replication found the same outcome (chatbot use predicts loneliness) but explicitly not the same mechanism (no effect on social connection), and the platform studied filed for Chapter 11 nine days before the paper’s own latest revision.
