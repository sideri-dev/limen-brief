Computer-use agents lose most of their reliability exactly where assistive technology forces them to operate.

A CHI 2026 dataset paper ran 16 participants — 8 sighted, 8 blind or low-vision — through 60 tasks and 158,325 logged events. Claude Sonnet 4.5's computer-use agent succeeded on 78.3% of tasks by default, already below the 99.16% human baseline, but the number that matters is what happens under the conditions blind and low-vision users actually run: 41.67% keyboard-only, and just 28.3% at 150% screen magnification, against 84.6% human success in that same condition.

Default-condition agent benchmarks simply don't test the modes assistive technology forces. The decision this changes is a pre-ship one — an accessibility-condition test pass belongs in the eval gate, not a patch after the first complaint.

When a computer-use agent fails a blind user, it's usually because it doesn't know it's done — not because it can't see the screen.

A separate three-week diary study put 8 blind users through 1,258 real commands across 12 applications with a computer-use prototype alongside their normal screen reader. The best of five models, GPT-5, reached only 52.5% success; tracing failures found the dominant modes were grounding, planning, constraint-tracking, and termination — not recognizing when a task was finished or a constraint honored.

That argues for a specific missing piece of agentic UI: an explicit "am I done, did I respect the constraint" checkpoint, distinct from a generic stop button. Published independently, the same week, with different authors and models than the item above — two studies converging on the same structural gap, not one finding restated.

AI coaches talk more and explain less than human experts do — and that trains passive users.

A study of 72 expert-novice coaching sessions (22,752 dialogue turns, 28.1 hours) on computer-use tasks compared human and LLM coaches. The LLMs gave more direct instructions but fewer explanations, error diagnoses, and knowledge-check questions, and were poorly grounded in what was actually visible on screen — learners passively followed instructions rather than engaging with the task.

Optimizing an AI coaching or help feature purely for instruction-completion speed trades away the skill transfer that's the point of coaching in the first place.

arXiv 2606.31980 (EMNLP 2026) →

Building difficulty-aware error disclosure for AI tools might be solving a problem people don't actually have.

A preregistered experiment with 577 participants tested whether AI errors on easy tasks bother people more than errors on hard tasks — the assumption behind a lot of confidence-UI design. Across error rates of 10/30/50% on diagram-generation tasks, more errors reduced use, but easy-task errors didn't reduce use any more than hard-task errors did.

That kills the assumption that a difficulty-aware confidence display will out-calibrate simple error-rate disclosure by default — that investment needs its own test before it's built.

Claude Fable 5.1 removed the "fast, no-think" mode entirely — thinking is now always on.

Anthropic's release notes, an independently maintained AWS Bedrock model card, and Simon Willison's own timed testing confirm the same thing from three angles: adaptive thinking on Fable 5.1 cannot be disabled, only dialed across five effort levels (low, medium, high, xhigh, max), and the cheapest setting still takes roughly 24 seconds. Willison's low-effort run took 23.8 seconds and 10 cents; max ran nearly 14 minutes and $3.30.

Pricing held flat at $10/$50 per million tokens, cache reads down to $0.25. Any interface built on this model must now expose or route that effort dial itself — it's the latency-and-cost budget control, not an implementation detail.
