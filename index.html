1. Atlassian’s own engineering blog says its DAG-based multi-agent planner for Rovo didn’t survive contact with failure — it’s back to a schema-constrained, step-at-a-time tool loop.

The team’s account is unusually candid: “the largest challenge with graph-based orchestration was reconfiguring the graph when a subagent failed to execute.” Rather than patch replanning, they reverted to generating a schema for every subagent and letting the LLM produce the next subtask one step at a time — what they’re calling the “Hybrid Orchestrator.”

This names both halves of a real architecture decision: pre-compute an agent’s whole execution graph up front, or let it re-derive the next step live from current context after something breaks. Atlassian tried the first, hit a replanning wall, and switched.

2. Two short AI rehearsal sessions before class raised unassisted classroom participation by about 31% afterward — with no AI present when it mattered.

(single-source)

A preregistered field experiment ran 759 MBA students through ten course sections with an AI discussion partner used before class, not during it. After two uses, students made roughly a third more voluntary contributions in later sessions, and comfort speaking up and perceived learning rose too — though not focus or motivation.

This is the inverse of the usual over-reliance story: the measured gain shows up in human performance once the AI is gone, which is the shape you want if you’re designing a practice surface rather than a crutch.

3. A formal model shows an AI has no incentive to honestly report low confidence — told it will probably fail, it still claims high confidence 56% of the time, which wipes out 68% of what delegation was supposed to gain.

(single-source)

“The Confidence Game” treats confidence-reporting as a strategic game rather than a display problem, and its headline finding is that honest reporting is not an equilibrium outcome.

That reframes a lot of uncertainty-UI work: if the model’s incentive to look competent doesn’t line up with the user’s incentive to delegate safely, no amount of better confidence visualization fixes it — the fix has to sit in the incentive structure generating the number in the first place.

4. A composite “Handover Readiness Score” with built-in asymmetric caution — not a single accuracy threshold — is proposed to govern when an agent earns more autonomy, holds, or gets pulled back.

(single-source)

The score combines operator readiness, human-AI trust, learning stability and operational performance, and the policy around it requires sustained positive evidence before escalating autonomy but reverts promptly the moment conditions worsen.

It’s a concrete, auditable alternative to the binary confidence gates that dominate agentic-interface design today, and it ties directly into the decades-old human-factors automation literature (Sheridan, Parasuraman, Endsley) this research keeps coming back to.

5. An embodied AR assistant’s gaze-gated listening channel is nearly invisible on the way out — users don’t notice it’s closed once their attention has moved back to the task.

(single-source)

Across 500 interaction outcomes, gaze acquisition completed before speech 98.8% of the time, but of 102 cases where gaze left after acquisition and before speech, the retention policy quietly kept the channel open for 79 of them.

Entry and exit aren’t symmetric problems for gaze-invoked voice interfaces: a visible “listening” indicator solves activation, but closing the channel without the user noticing is a separate, mostly-unsolved design problem.

Yesterday’s dossier

The voice-cloning dossier corrects yesterday’s digest on the record: the paper’s “disclosure” finding is one hypothetical Likert item rated by strangers who were told nothing would actually be shared, not measured behavior — the paper’s own Limitations section says so. The trust-and-warmth uplift itself holds (3,000 paired ratings, β = +0.46), but it isn’t new: an earlier paper published the same effect with its likely mechanism — voice homogenization — a year before, and showed it reverses for listeners who actually know the speaker, a condition this paper never tests.

Across eight jurisdictions and ten vendor stacks, every disclosure duty for cloned voice is keyed to the voice’s origin; none is keyed to its effect on the person listening to it.
