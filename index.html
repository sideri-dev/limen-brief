Euro NCAP's 2026 protocol makes menu-based touch input more than two steps an automatic fail for nine driving functions — and manufacturers are visibly reversing course.

For nine safety-critical functions — indicators, gear selector, hazard lights, eCall, horn, wipers, full beam, vehicle assistance — the protocol forbids burying the control more than two menu taps deep: "a Function implementation which consists of a Menu-based touch input (>2 steps) is never acceptable." In force since Jan 1, 2026; it scores the worst qualifying path, not the best, so a good shortcut doesn't rescue a bad fallback.

Mercedes CEO Ola Källenius already says the industry "might have gone a little too far" toward digital controls, promising "the most sensible buttons" back and fitting a hard-key strip to its new electric GLC. For anyone specifying in-car or in-home HMI, this is a binding floor.

A memory feature can ace a recall-accuracy benchmark and still fail the thing users actually notice — the gap measured 71 points.

A four-month deployment (40 users, 1,872 sessions, seven memory conditions) found direct-QA recall accuracy ranged from 19.7% to 70.1% across conditions, but satisfaction didn't move at all. Holding model and context fixed, the same system that scored 78.8% when asked directly about a fact volunteered only 7.9% of it unprompted in conversation — a 71-point gap between what memory "knows" and what it surfaces.

Only natural-integration, not QA-accuracy, correlated with satisfaction: test whether memory surfaces itself, not just whether it can be interrogated.

Handing an AI the final call changes what it's willing to commit to — even when its rule-following is otherwise perfect.

A controlled audit of three LLMs found that when two applicable rules pointed to different actions, the models "almost never committed when users retained authority, but committed in every delegated case," despite executing both rules perfectly when directly instructed.

The divergence lives entirely in authority framing, not capability — a control-surface decision for multi-stakeholder agent features: does the user keep the tie-break, or hand it off. Testable per product, independent of the model.

A webcam-driven tutoring interface silently swaps its own explanation style mid-conversation based on inferred confusion or boredom — and no one could tell.

Aura, from ETH Zürich, infers boredom, confusion, frustration or engagement from facial expression and hot-swaps its response style in under 10ms at sentence boundaries, undisclosed. In a 20-person study against GPT-4o and Llama-3-8B, its learning-gain edge holds against Llama-3 but is statistically tied with GPT-4o once the full comparison runs; the cleaner win is speed — 73 seconds per task against GPT-4o's 93 and Llama-3's 100.

More telling: asked directly, no participant reported noticing an abrupt shift, and 11 of 20 still ranked Aura first. Strong evidence that undetected mid-generation adaptation is achievable and preferred in aggregate — making "the user should always tell it changed" a design choice being traded away for efficiency, not a free assumption.

A position paper names eight concrete interface mechanisms that actually preserve human oversight of AI agents, not just the gesture of a human in the loop.

Its core claim is structural: "the very act of being an overseer degrades the capacities oversight requires" — oversight design has to fight the erosion of the overseer's own judgment, not just police the AI. Its Section 5 taxonomy is genuinely usable: delay-and-choice mechanisms (let the user decide whether and when to see the AI's output), batch review (one diff for a completed unit, not action-by-action), and override-rate monitoring as a proxy for creeping approval fatigue.

No empirical study backs it — cite the argument, not the evidence — but each mechanism is a decision surface for agent approval flows.

Yesterday's dossier

AI emotional support and the choice moment: what the congruence result actually establishes, and what no shipped product or statute does about it.

The headline claim — AI support rates superior only when chosen — rests on a design where choice was self-selected and only assignment congruence was randomized, so choosers of AI and humans arrive with very different priors and "forced routing degrades quality" doesn't hold up. The cleaner result: one randomized 5-minute AI conversation raised future AI-choice from 45.5% to 70.1% regardless of what the person originally wanted, while an LLM-judge found no actual empathy difference between congruent and incongruent conversations — the effect is interpretive, not behavioral.

The 28-day field study reanalyzes the existing Fang et al. 2025 MIT×OpenAI dataset, not new data, and its abstract undersells a significant open-ended shift the Discussion concedes. Shipped practice already sits on the other side of the choice variable — OpenAI, Character.AI, Meta and Wysa route or price around choice rather than expose it — and none of the five statutes in force regulates choice, only disclosure and prohibition.
