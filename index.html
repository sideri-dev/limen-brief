A two-week CHI 2026 study of 19 screenwriters finds they actively shape how AI enters their process, not just react to it.

Tang, Zhou, Li, Xie, Ma and Qu framed the study through Bandura's theory of human agency and found screenwriters "not only mindfully planned, foresaw, and responded to AI usage, but... developed themselves and human-AI co-creation paradigms."

That argues for building co-creation tools around a workflow that evolves across a project's life, not a static assistance mode. The sample is likely Hong Kong/China-based (four of six authors at HKUST, two at Microsoft Research), though the paper states no participant nationality, so treat that as probable, not confirmed.

Independent measurement finds Claude's long-context latency scales linearly while GPT-5.6's scales quadratically.

Epoch AI's own benchmark — independent of either vendor — put a number on it: at 10 million tokens of context, the marginal time-to-first-token for an extra 10,000 tokens is 1.66s (GPT-5.6 Terra) and 2.20s (GPT-5.6 Sol) against 0.129s (Claude Sonnet 5) and 0.218s (Claude Opus 5). Sonnet 5 "stayed close to linear scaling with no observable curvature," while Opus was noisier but still linear.

That's a real latency-budget decision: a long-context feature that reads as instant on one model family degrades unpredictably on another, so model choice for a long-context product is a UX decision, not just a cost one.

A usability study of Operator and Manus finds agents fail from missing meta-cognition, not missing capability.

Shome, Krishnan and Das first mapped 102 commercial AI agents into three categories (orchestration, creation, insight), then ran a usability study (N=31) on Operator and Manus, finding "agent capabilities that were misaligned with user mental models" and "agents lacking the meta-cognitive abilities necessary for effective collaboration" — the agents don't know what they don't know, and don't say so.

That argues for meta-cognitive disclosure as a first-class affordance, not the fallback error state it is today. Preprint, single-source.

Anthropic's Claude Managed Agents can now let the server, not your code, decide whether a tool call runs.

A September 10 release note adds an "auto" permission policy: "the server evaluates each agent or MCP tool call and runs it, denies it, or pauses for your approval," with an evaluation field on every tool-use event.

That decouples per-call risk evaluation from any local runtime — the question shifts from "ask every time" vs. "never ask" to tuning what the evaluator treats as reversible.

Yesterday's dossier

The caption-metrics paper doesn't show quality metrics fail on ASR captions — it shows they degrade by roughly half and bias in opposite directions per metric, with WER and ACE2 flattering ASR captions while NER over-corrects, even though viewers rated the two sources the same. The comparison isn't human-vs-machine either: the authors don't know which TV captions were steno, re-speech, or ASR.

The finding that actually carries a design decision is latency, not accuracy — removing TV's 8.46s delay bought back more rating points than removing ASR's 2s delay, and NHK has already shipped a delay-based fix since 2021. No regulator numerically mandates caption accuracy, though two live US proceedings are building toward one. The original prescription — test with DHH raters per generation method — holds, and is shipped by nobody today, including these authors, whose own Limitations call their method "clearly not sustainable."
