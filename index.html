1. A six-month cohort of 673 Chinese AI users shows two companion-bonding profiles that look identical until you check for harm.

Study 1 interviewed 52 users to build the taxonomy; Study 2 followed 673 people for six months and sorted them into four impact profiles. Two — “mixed impact” and “risk-driven impact” — run equally high on affective bonding, but only the risk-driven group shows greater need frustration, emotion-regulation difficulty, depressive/anxiety symptoms, and lower self-esteem and flourishing.

That kills the simple safety question (“does bonding predict harm?”) and replaces it with the actionable one: detect which profile a user is trending into and intervene there, not at the bonding metric itself. It’s also the first non-Western longitudinal companion-AI sample this corpus has found since the hunt was requested.

2. Users say they’d rather get a wrong answer than hear “I can’t help with that” too often.

A 599-person study found people most satisfied with genuine responses, next-most with hallucinations, least with refusals — despite knowing the hallucinations were less accurate. Explanations helped satisfaction with occasional refusals but not frequent ones, and high-need-for-closure users rated refusal-heavy systems worse across the board.

This undercuts “refuse when uncertain” as a neutral safe default: refusal-rate tuning is a satisfaction-versus-safety tradeoff a client has to consciously own.

3. Letting people build an analysis piece by piece beats handing them a finished one, even when both cover the same ground.

An 800-person, four-condition randomized experiment compared chat-based analysis against workflows scaffolded around how professionals actually structure a task. The scaffolded versions improved coverage over plain chat, and the user-directed variant additionally produced a broader range of analytic requests and lower subjective effort.

The fork is concrete — ship a finished analysis, or scaffold an incremental, user-steered build — and incremental wins on effort and analytic range at matched output quality, arguing against defaulting to “just give me the answer” UX.

4. Getting people to judge their own AI-assisted work correctly and getting them to perform better are two different design problems — and most interventions only move one.

Across 917 participants and 12 tasks, only two of four calibration interventions — reliability cards and contrasting replies — reduced estimation error and overconfidence and improved confidence discrimination; none produced a measurable task-performance gain.

That separation matters for scoping a feature: a reliability-card UI is a trust-calibration play, not a productivity one, and selling it as the latter disappoints whoever measures the wrong outcome.

5. Forcing people to consult the model on every task only captures about half of what the model is actually better at — and makes people worse judges of their own answers.

A 535-person study across a 40-item reasoning battery and four models found mandatory consultation captured roughly half the model’s own accuracy edge, and post-advice confidence distinguished correct from incorrect answers less reliably than unaided confidence did.

An argument against always-on, mandatory-consult advice surfaces: selective, optional deference captures more of the available gain without degrading judgment as much as forcing it does.

Yesterday’s dossier

The digest’s #1 item from 2026-09-15 — “developers control rather than delegate to coding agents” — mostly holds, but not as cleanly as the headline suggested. The paper’s own numbers show 31% of participants (3 of 13) didn’t review every change, and those three were working outside their professional domain, so control is domain-contingent, not categorical. Corroboration runs along a clean self-report-versus-behavior fault line: self-report sources agree experts control their agents, behavioral data (PR-intervention rates, sabotage-detection, screen-recording time) shows substantially less review than people claim. Four control models now ship side by side across major coding tools, interruption budget a designed choice. Open question: whether “control not delegation” is driven by expertise or by how verifiable the work is — confounded in every study so far.
