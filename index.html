1. A migrating dialogue agent stays “the same” across devices only when its voice and speaking style stay consistent — not when the hardware looks alike.

(single-source — Human Interface Society journal, Japan)

A one-day-old Japanese HIS journal paper runs two experiments on an agent that moves between a robot and a pair of earphones. Hold the physical appearance constant and change voice, speaking style or conversational content, and identity perception drops — any one of the three is enough.

But when voice and speaking style are kept and the conversation doesn’t contradict itself, people perceive it as the same agent across two devices that don’t look alike at all. For anyone building a persona meant to span a robot, earbuds and a phone app, the lever that matters is the voice and the conversation, not matching the casing.

2. AI clones of museum science communicators got measurably better by their own creators’ judgment — and visitor ratings barely moved.

(single-source — ACM CUI 2026 Best Paper)

A five-day field deployment put AI Clones of six professional science communicators in a museum, running over 450 conversation sessions. The source performers rated their own clones significantly improved on nine of ten communication dimensions.

Visitors rating the same clones saw no overall change, reaching significance for only one of six clones after correction. The trap generalizes past this paper: a creator’s self-review of their own AI clone improving tells you nothing about whether the audience it’s actually for noticed.

3. Deployed support agents almost never get a second chance to repair a conversation.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, CMU / UC Santa Cruz)

Measured against a Reddit human-conversation baseline, one production pipeline’s agents see the original user return to continue a thread 1.2% of the time versus 40.9% for humans, and multi-turn continuation after a correction is nearly absent (under 0.1% vs. 38.5%).

The researchers are careful to say this describes one deployed pipeline, not agents in general — but the mechanism is instructive: if the platform gives no return path for a second try, repair can’t happen no matter how capable the model is underneath.

4. GitHub’s own postmortem: giving Copilot better review tools made its code reviews worse, and the fix was rewriting the instructions, not the tools.

(vendor-primary — GitHub Blog)

“The tools weren’t the problem. The instructions were,” GitHub writes, after tool access alone regressed review quality. Rewriting the focused tool instructions cut average review cost roughly 20%.

The more useful part is the negative control they report on themselves: the same instruction fix tried in the CLI “did not produce the same kind of win” — a stated reminder that a design fix proven on one surface doesn’t automatically transfer to another.

5. Giving an agent its own operation IDs doesn’t actually stop duplicate actions from causing harm.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A new measurement paper finds roughly a quarter of agent executions need recovery, and 30 of 143 confirmed partial executions end up followed by a harmful recovery attempt.

The part worth remembering: identifier-based duplicate protection — the model tagging each action so a repeat can be recognized and skipped — doesn’t detectably reduce harm, because the model just issues a new identifier for what is, in reality, the same operation. Duplicate-call detectors catch three-quarters of duplicates but only two-fifths of the actual harm.
