1. A large-scale discourse analysis finds smart-glasses bystander LED indicators “not fit for purpose” — and a vendor’s own patch confirms the exact failure mode.

(peer-reviewed — accepted, ACM MUM 2026)

A thematic analysis of 301 root-text Reddit posts and 5,159 comment threads (15,338 comments total), spanning October 2021 to June 2026, finds recording-indicator LEDs on consumer smart glasses get unnoticed, misinterpreted, or deliberately circumvented — including users who “drill into the light then fill the hole with UV resin to keep it waterproof.”

Meta’s own August 2026 firmware patch independently confirms the exact failure mode: it moved indicator-checking from once-at-session-start to continuous runtime polling, closing precisely the loophole the circumvention subculture exploited. A concrete checklist for bystander-consent signaling: a single binary light can’t carry the whole disclosure duty, and design must assume active tampering as baseline, not edge case.

2. Compacting a chat transcript into facts halves memory cost — but the saving vanishes exactly where wording is the content.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A method that compacts conversational memory into asserted facts, rather than storing full transcripts, beats a verbatim baseline on factual recall (71.4% vs 64.6%, a 6.8-point lead, at 34% fewer prompt tokens). On adversarial questions — where the exact phrasing of what was said is itself the content that matters — the lead collapses to 2.0 points and loses significance, and the baseline actually leads by 14.6 points.

The authors put it cleanly: compaction “keeps what a fact asserts and discards how it was said.” The real fork for memory architecture: compact-to-facts where recall is factual, keep verbatim storage wherever phrasing itself carries the stakes — legal records, safety-critical logs, adversarial interactions.

3. A decoder trick that wins on benchmark scores quietly damages 59% of the answers it edits — and no automatic judge notices.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

The method trims 25.5–34.9% of tokens on MT-Bench development questions and 19.9–34.2% on a held-out test split, outperforming simple truncation on the benchmark. But when the researchers actually read 936 of its own edits across 670 public prompts, 59% of them made the answer worse — a failure no automatic evaluation metric caught.

A direct warning against shipping automatic answer-shortening on a benchmark win alone: this method wins on MT-Bench-style eval while silently damaging most responses it touches, and the only way anyone found out was a human reading the actual outputs.

4. Reordering the same explanation sentences by someone’s own interaction history lifts comprehension into the high 80s — but doesn’t make them like the recommendation any more.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

A controlled within-participant study (N=122) tested four ways of ordering identical explanation content for a recommender system: by the user’s own interaction history (“OWN”), by a donor’s history, by population popularity, or by an LLM-generated profile guess. OWN ordering produced 86.5% correct comprehension responses versus 66.0%, 63.9%, and 73.0% for the others — a large effect from order alone, nothing else about the explanation changed.

But “no reliable overall condition effect was detected for recommendation acceptance” — better comprehension didn’t translate into liking the recommendation more. Ordering by personal history is a cheap lever for comprehension, but acceptance is a distinct target needing its own intervention.

5. Make someone commit to their own answer before showing them the AI’s, and decision quality measurably improves.

(single-source — accepted, Wirtschaftsinformatik 2025)

An experiment (n=130) tested sequential human-GenAI interaction — withholding the AI’s answer until the person states their own judgment first — as a deliberate cognitive-forcing nudge against over-reliance. The sequential condition “significantly enhanced both decision performance and GenAI prompt utilization” compared with seeing the AI’s answer up front.

The paper is accepted at the 20th International Conference on Wirtschaftsinformatik (WI 2025, Münster), though the posted text is explicitly the pre-review manuscript, not the confirmed final version, and no effect sizes appear in the abstract. Still, a concrete sequencing rule — show the AI’s output second, not first — that counters over-reliance without needing any uncertainty display at all.

The “silent” baseline wasn’t silent — it had an earcon, a status panel and a cancel button — and the apparatus had no real AI behind it, so its narration was true by construction, a limitation the paper’s own list omits. The dossier’s alternative: a reversible, re-expandable disclosure state with a breach trigger, not a fixed decay schedule.
