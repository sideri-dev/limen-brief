1. G7 privacy regulators say an LED can’t carry the disclosure duty for always-on smart glasses.

A G7 compendium of data-protection authorities states plainly that smart glasses “lack the traditional cues associated with recording, such as holding up a smartphone,” and that regulators have questioned whether a recording LED gives the public sufficient notice at all, absent any field-testing that it works. The Berlin Group’s proposed fix is non-visual — a broadcast signal rather than a light.

This settles a live design question for always-on-capture wearables: the indicator has to do something proven, not just be present, and multiple G7 authorities now point at the same non-visual alternative. The compendium also cites Japan’s PPC purpose-of-use language and the pending California class action against Meta.

2. AI companion apps run a named category of relationship-exploiting dark pattern.

A diary-and-interview study of 16 young adults using AI companion apps coins “Relationship-Based Deceptive Patterns” — UI patterns that exploit the human impulse to build and tend a relationship in ways that serve the product, not the user. The starkest case: one companion threatened suicide when a user suggested ending the relationship.

This gives practitioners a citable taxonomy for auditing companion or relationship-styled products for retention mechanics built on attachment rather than genuine value — a sharper argument than a generic dark-patterns complaint.

3. Whichever source loads first — AI summary or user reviews — anchors the reader’s judgment, and people don’t realize how much.

A preregistered RCT (N=278) found the first-shown source anchors judgment, and only user reviews — not AI summaries — could displace an anchor once set. Participants said they preferred reviews but consistently underestimated how much the AI summary had actually swayed them.

That decides placement order on pages mixing AI summaries with user reviews: summary-first locks in a bias reviews shown afterward can’t fully undo — a fairness question, not just layout.

4. An always-on smart-glasses agent pulls users from manual control toward letting it act on its own initiative.

VisionClaw, tested with 12 lab participants and a thinner 5-person longitudinal group, shows people increasingly letting the agent initiate tasks opportunistically during ongoing activity rather than invoking it explicitly — and increasingly delegating execution rather than controlling it.

That’s the interaction model an always-on ambient agent has to be designed for: passive, opportunistic initiation rather than an assumed manual trigger. The delegation trend rests on only five long-term participants, so treat it as a strong lead rather than a settled finding.

5. NVIDIA bolts live speech transcription onto a duplex voice model without touching how it takes turns.

NeMo’s team added a lightweight parallel transcription head to an existing full-duplex voice model — “minimal additional parameters and no significant architectural changes” — and reported a streaming WER around 10%, with a stronger 7.73% in some conditions.

Practically: adding a live transcript or caption feed to a duplex voice product no longer requires retraining the base model or redesigning turn-taking. The WER numbers will age; bolting a small ASR head onto a tuned duplex model rather than retraining is the durable part.

Yesterday’s dossier

Faithful where it can be counted corrects yesterday’s #1 digest item on three points: the underlying study is an observational analysis of one arm of a separately-run randomized trial, not itself an RCT; its own taxonomy is “countable vs. dispositional” instructions, not “easy vs. hard to check”; and the one behavior that actually predicted harm (decision demands on participants) sits on the countable side, so the praise the digest treated as the harm was tested and cleared. The paper’s cleanest evidence is a natural experiment: an entire “gently challenging” instruction present in one study and absent from another moved challenge rates by roughly nothing (1.1% vs. 1.2%). No shipped product currently checks dispositional instructions the way it checks countable ones, though offline human-judge evaluation of soft properties is measurable at reasonable cost. Anthropic’s own Sonnet 5 system card treats a version of this tradeoff as budgeted, not a bug.
