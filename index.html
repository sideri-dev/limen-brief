A trust dip after the honeymoon period isn't a design failure — five sessions in, it's the normal curve.

A CHI 2026 within-subjects study ran 12 professional UX evaluators through five sessions each (~6 hours total) with novice- and expert-like conversational assistants. Trust followed an initial novelty spike, then a dip, then recovery — and only after that recovery did evaluators rate the more capable assistant significantly more efficient, trustworthy and comprehensive, despite not noticing any expertise difference early on.

A single-session pilot would have caught the dip and missed the recovery; the practical consequence is when in a rollout you actually run trust and reliance evaluation.

A robot that never talks to you turns out to be better at introducing two strangers than one that does.

HRI '26's Best Design Paper put 32 participants through 16 stranger pairings in a shared waiting room, using mirrors to redirect mutual gaze instead of a talking social robot. In 12 of the 16 groups, the first meaningful contact between the strangers happened through the mirrors, not face-to-face.

The lead author's framing is the transferable design principle: "the focus is not on the robot — it's on the other human," and social technology needs to know when to step back, not just how to initiate.

What developers say AI coding tools do for them, and what their own telemetry shows, are two different stories.

An ICSE '26 study logged 151.9 million IDE events across 800 JetBrains developers (400 AI users, 400 non-users) over two years. 82.3% self-reported a productivity gain, but the behavioral signal tells a sharper story: AI users added 587 characters a month against non-users' 75, and deleted 102 a month against 7.6.

Satisfaction surveys and churn/deletion telemetry are measuring different things once the novelty period ends — the churn signal is the one to instrument if the goal is calibrated reliance rather than a good survey score.

People blame a lying robot exactly as much as a lying human — a Japanese replication closes off a design excuse.

A Japanese HAI Symposium study (N=201) replicated Kneer's 2021 "Can a Robot Lie?" design and found the type of agent made no significant difference to how much blame participants assigned; only whether the agent actually lied did. The paper's own language is direct: whether the actor is human or robot, the act of lying itself is what draws the blame.

That closes off a design assumption worth checking before it ships — that labeling a wrong or evasive AI output as "just an AI" will soften how much a user blames it for the error.

A cleaning robot can navigate an airport perfectly and still read as rude, because rudeness isn't a navigation problem.

A CHI 2026 video-analysis study of cleaning robots at a major commercial airport found the robots were technically proficient at obstacle avoidance but still disrupted the social order of the space — they couldn't read mutual adjustment between people, recognize a social group as a unit, or understand what a location was for.

The authors argue movement through shared space is "an interactional and collaborative accomplishment," not just a path-planning problem. A path-planner scored only on completion and collision-avoidance is being scored on the wrong axis for any robot sharing space with people.

Every alternative reading is still worse than the published number, though, and the ordering (default beats keyboard-only beats magnifier) survives all of them, so the qualitative finding stands even though its magnitude doesn't — it should carry [CONFLICT], not just [SINGLE-SOURCE].

The digest's claim that 150% magnification is what users "actually use" isn't the paper's own framing and isn't supported by the only survey data available (WebAIM 2018: 47.6% of low-vision respondents magnify at 200% or above). The durable finding is structural: none of seven major computer-use benchmarks varies an accessibility condition, and three vendors' own computer-use documentation contains zero references to accessibility or assistive technology — while GitHub's Copilot CLI already publishes a conformance report naming exactly those test conditions.
