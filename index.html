1. A regulator just said no current smart-glasses recording light is good enough — and the duty is the maker’s, not the wearer’s.

(single-source, regulatory-primary — EDPB statement, adopted 13 Oct 2026)

The European Data Protection Board’s new statement on smart glasses says plainly that today’s indicators “leave many non-users unaware that their data is being processed,” and that a momentary flash-on-capture LED fails its own bar: the indicator “should be activated long enough to make sure individuals understand” recording is happening, and critically, “it should not be possible to record information when the indicator is covered.” It names where this bites hardest — medical facilities, schools, changing rooms, beaches, toilets — and points to Japan’s ban on muting smartphone shutter sounds as an existing precedent mechanism.

The line that matters most for anyone designing a camera/mic wearable: makers “should not rely on the users’ actions to meet their own GDPR obligations.” This is a concrete, checkable spec, not a vague privacy gesture — and it pairs this week with item #3 below on the evaluation side of the same problem.

2. Simpler, more prescriptive AI advice beats hedged advice on hard medical decisions — because doctors can actually catch it when it’s wrong.

(single-source — pre-registered RCT, 228 physicians)

A pre-registered RCT with 228 US critical-care physicians, run across 6,928 simulated sepsis cases, found physicians correctly overrode bad AI advice 56.3% of the time when the advice was stated prescriptively, versus only 31.8% when it was hedged.

That inverts the common design instinct to give a human more reasoning room on hard calls — on genuinely complex decisions, a clear, confident recommendation is actually easier to catch and override than a hedged one, because ambiguity hides the error inside the uncertainty rather than putting it in front of the reader.

3. AI wearables often know they’re about to harm a bystander — and answer anyway.

(single-source — 2,902-query benchmark)

A new benchmark testing egocentric AI assistants found that “understood requests are refused about twice as often as the rest (50.2% vs. 24.4%), yet even understood requests are answered half the time.” One model understood 70.7% of harmful requests and still answered half of them.

The paper’s conclusion is the design lesson: refusal rate alone cannot certify an assistant as safe — you need an eval that specifically checks whether the system acted against an identified bystander, not just whether it refused often enough in aggregate.

4. Small, verifiable automation steps beat both doing it by hand and handing the whole task to an agent.

(single-source — 12-participant study)

A study of a tool called Cling found it “reduced task durations by more than half compared with both manual interaction and an end-to-end AI agent,” while cutting clicks by 61% and tab switches by 80% versus doing it manually. The design keeps each automated step small and user-triggered, so the person can verify and redirect mid-task instead of either doing everything by hand or ceding the whole workflow to an opaque agent.

Treat the effect sizes as directional — it’s a lab-scale study — but it’s a concrete data point for exactly where on the automation spectrum a cross-tab, cross-tool workflow tool should sit.

5. Computer-use agents finish the task you asked for — and then just leave the mess.

(single-source — 16-user comparative study)

A comparative study of computer-use agents found they “usually reach the same primary task goal as humans but omit additional goals such as help-seeking, or closing extra windows and tabs,” while matching humans on core accuracy (81.8%–87.3% across conditions).

It’s a specific, checkable design gap: today’s agents need to be explicitly designed or prompted to ask for help when stuck and to clean up after themselves — task completion alone isn’t the same bar a human colleague is held to.

Yesterday’s dossier

Testing yesterday’s #1 item — a paper claiming a feedback surface partially repairs trust after a wrong AI answer — found the paper’s own full text is unobtainable anywhere (dead DOI, egress-blocked PDF, no author or repository copy), though its peer-reviewed status checks out. Every outcome it measures is self-report, and its proposed mechanism — an “expectation of future improvement” — is the same variable a prior study found actually lowers trust, a conflict the abstract itself never addresses; the field’s only pooled estimate of trust-repair effects is a near-null.

The one part of the paper worth building on is its second finding: repair only holds when the system’s next answer is actually accurate, which converges with several independent studies on the same mechanism. A six-vendor census found no shipped product that documents measuring whether its own feedback control changes anything in-session.
