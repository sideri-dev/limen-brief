1. A miscalibrated AI confidence score is measurably worse than showing no confidence at all — well-calibrated confidence raised decision accuracy +20%, miscalibrated confidence raised it only +2%.

An AAAI-26 study (N=184, within-subjects, logic puzzles) isolated calibration as the variable: a confidence score that tracked the AI’s actual reliability measurably improved decisions; one that didn’t produced almost no gain, even though people still rated it as more useful when it looked confident. A confident-looking but wrong confidence score is actively deceptive, not merely unhelpful — the exact failure mode automation-bias research predicts.

Peer-reviewed, main-track AAAI, outside the lane’s usual venue list but comparably rigorous. Don’t ship a confidence indicator unless you’ve measured that it tracks reliability.

2. Scheduling when an AI assistant offers help, rather than making the user ask on demand, makes people rate it more positively — even with identical performance and less money kept.

A UC Santa Barbara study (ACM IUI ’26, N=66, Rush Hour puzzles with real monetary stakes) compared a pre-scheduled “Timer” mode against an on-demand “Button” mode. Task performance and ending budget were statistically identical — but Timer-mode participants still rated the AI more favorably, even when their budget was low.

Perceived AI quality decouples from both outcome and effort here, making delivery timing a free, swappable lever worth testing before assuming on-demand is the neutral default.

3. A mandatory human checkpoint on AI-generated research summaries didn’t stop over a third of users from saying they’d skip verification after a short interaction.

(single-source)

A 63-person survey of a tool forcing human review before accepting AI-generated literature-review synthesis found 42.9% rated its usability highly — and 34.9% said they’d trust AI summaries without further checking after brief use. The authors’ own framing is the finding: whether a checkpoint produces oversight depends on whether the user is willing to exercise it — a calibration problem design must address, not assume a gate solves.

Self-reported intention, not observed behavior, but a clean argument against treating “insert a review step” as a solved safeguard.

4. Adding gesture generation to a full-duplex voice model makes the speech it generates worse over time — the first reported cost of extending a shared-token-stream voice architecture to body motion.

(single-source, not peer-reviewed)

A Nagoya University team (with Aisin Corp.) extended the J-Moshi duplex voice architecture from 17 to 33 layers, adding a VQ-VAE “posture” channel for synchronized body motion. Motion tracked reasonably well (mean joint error 38.7mm), but the added modality degraded voice output over time — early generation stayed grammatical, then language structure broke down, and motion collapsed toward static training poses.

Anyone extending a Moshi-style duplex model with gesture should budget for a speech-quality cost by default, not treat it as free.

5. OpenAI’s Agents API approval dialog for computer-use agents only gates which websites the agent can reach — not what it does once there, so teams must build a second confirmation layer themselves.

OpenAI’s docs are explicit: “the browser requires the user’s approval before accessing each new website origin,” but “origin approval does not enforce confirmation before individual actions” — per-action confirmation depends on the agent actually calling a function built for that purpose. OpenAI’s own mitigation: if an app must guarantee confirmation before a purchase or destructive change, restrict the agent’s browser to resources that can’t do those things at all.

Worth knowing before assuming an origin-approval prompt doubles as an action-level safeguard.

Yesterday’s dossier

Anthropic’s own autonomy-telemetry post turns out to mix evidentiary tiers that look like one number: the real, solid finding is a mechanical drift (auto-approve rising, keypress interrupts rising too), but the headline 73%/0.8% figures rest on a classifier with only 46% precision, and the “accumulating trust” story is actually a cross-sectional cohort effect dressed as an individual trajectory.

The post’s prescription — “visibility plus simple intervention” — names a view and a lever but no trigger, which is exactly what 1983 automation-safety literature says is the missing load-bearing piece, and no product checked (including Anthropic’s own) fires an alert on the risk of an action before it happens; every alert fires on completion, blocking, or elapsed time. What Anthropic actually shipped in place of a human approval modal was a second AI model reviewing actions, not a watching human, and its own telemetry schema never tracked that substitution.
