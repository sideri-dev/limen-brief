1. Claude Duet’s approval prompts are calibrated against approval fatigue, not just against risk.

(vendor-primary — Anthropic’s own account, Oct 21 & 23, 2026)

Before Duet can send, pay, sign or delete anything, it stops and shows exactly what it’s about to do — but that isn’t a universal “approve once” filter, since some steps aren’t covered by a saved rule no matter how often you’ve approved them. Anthropic says plainly it spent real time tuning request frequency against approval fatigue: “too many requests teach people to tap through them, sometimes without adequate consideration.”

A separate, out-of-model check screens for sensitive data, but Anthropic admits it “will occasionally miss something or flag a harmless message” — paired with human approval, not trusted alone. Purchases use single-merchant, single-amount one-time cards as a structural backstop.

2. No commercial LLM deployment can currently promise byte-identical output on a repeated request — only a fully pinned serving pipeline can.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, 14 deployments tested)

A single researcher sent the same request repeatedly to 14 commercial deployments, each set to the deterministic sampling it offered; every deployment returned a differing assessment at least once, and in twelve the label changed.

The only setup that produced byte-identical reruns — across interruptions, edits, a different machine, even a later day — pinned OCR, chunking, embedding, indexing, retrieval and inference together; temperature-0/fixed-seed settings alone did nothing, because batching and GPU/scheduler variance move the output underneath them. For any interface claiming reproducible output, the fix sits in the serving pipeline, not a model parameter.

(vendor-primary — Figma Blog, published Oct 22, 2026)

4. Memory-based and survey-based chatbot personalization trade off in opposite directions — there’s no free win here, only a choice of failure mode.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, Google DeepMind, 992 participants)

In a 992-participant, 5-day field study, people given memory-based personalization rated the chatbot significantly less “creepy” than controls — inferred personalization reads as less invasive than being asked outright. People given survey-based personalization instead reported marginally higher regret over what they’d disclosed, and leaned measurably more on the chatbot over friends and experts afterward.

The hedge, “marginally,” belongs in this story — any product adding memory or an explicit profile step should expect to inherit one of these costs, not neither.

5. For EMG wearable control, the accuracy number calibration improves doesn’t predict the usability number a user actually feels.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, 16-participant closed-loop study)

Three calibrated controllers hit much higher offline balanced accuracy than six calibration-free ones (90.6% vs. 73.1% for the best of each kind), but in a closed-loop task with 16 participants completion rates didn’t differ significantly — and the single best completion rate came from a calibration-free controller.

The authors are blunt: “offline accuracy did not predict completion rate across controllers or within participants,” so the number calibration improves isn’t evidence of a benefit a user actually experiences. That reframes what a wearable-control team should validate before keeping a calibration step at all — closed-loop task success, not offline accuracy.
