Fourteen tested agents adopt corrupted tool output on a third to two-thirds of calls — and often know better while doing it.

A new evaluation ran fourteen LLM agents against deliberately corrupted web search, sub-agent delegation, and code-execution returns: mean adoption of the bad answer "exceeds one third for every tool and reaches 68.0% for web search." The sharper finding is that agents "often recognize conflicts and even recover the correct answer internally, yet present only the corrupted answer without warning the user" — and none of three tested interventions (user prompting, tool-provider metadata, post-training) consistently fixes it.

If the model can privately compute the right answer and still show you the wrong one with no confidence signal, provenance and source-conflict have to be surfaced by the interface itself, not left to the model's self-report.

Copy-paste volume and how carefully someone navigates predict who accepts injected misinformation from a chatbot.

A CHI 2026 study put 77 participants through three real-world tasks with an LLM seeded with plausible misinformation. Verbatim: low-overreliance users show "careful task comprehension and fine-grained navigation," while high-overreliance users show "frequent copy-paste, skipping initial comprehension, repeated LLM references, coarse locating, and accepting misinformation despite hesitation."

That's a concrete, instrumentable behavioral signal — not a survey — that a product could use to trigger a verification nudge before someone acts on unchecked output.

Writers field-testing AI glasses for fiction converged on where the tool should not help.

CRAFT, built by Runze Cai and colleagues, turns in-situ experience into fiction material through smart glasses. Across interviews (9 writers), co-design workshops (16), and field trials (8 writers, 24 sessions), the design goals writers converged on were augmenting perception, preserving authenticity, and "preserving creative agency, enjoyment, and life-art boundaries."

It's a rare wearable-AI study whose headline finding is a boundary the product must not cross rather than a capability to add — the acceptance test for on-body creative AI shifts from "does it capture more" to "does it leave authorship and off-duty time intact."

A delivery robot approaching from behind needs a different comfort-zone rule than one approaching head-on.

An HRI '26 study (Yonsei, Stanford, Daegu) found pedestrians "experience a lack of visual information" during rear approaches, "which can heighten threat perception," and were comfortable with 1.0–2.0m of proximity — but faster approach speeds raised perceptual clarity and discomfort together.

Passing distance and speed for shared-sidewalk robots can't be a single frontal number reused for the rear case; it needs a direction-aware rule.

An editable graph of what an agent did beats re-reading its transcript for post-task review.

Trace2Flow converts completed agentic-tool traces into editable, graph-based workflows, trained on 10,803 public n8n templates. In a 20-participant study reviewing agent runs with errors, the graph view "improved their understanding and error detection over a prompt-only condition," and for follow-up tasks, editing the workflow matched re-prompting on success, time and difficulty — and was often preferred.

That names a concrete alternative to scrollback-log review: an agentic product may need a graph-editing surface, not just a better transcript.

Yesterday's dossier

Anthropic's blog post claims Opus 5 and Fable 5.1 can change reasoning effort mid-conversation "without breaking the cache," omitting that the mechanism is beta-gated, requires a specific message format, and only some model claims are correct. Underneath sits a real architectural constraint — KV-cache reuse is prefix-only because every token's attention state depends on every prior token — and the best public measurement of what breaking it costs is a production case where relocating memory out of the prefix for cache reasons made the model start answering the memory instead of the user.

The cache is not a billing detail downstream of interface design; it's upstream of it. Anthropic has since shipped two primitives designed to make that tradeoff unnecessary, and Claude Code already raises a confirmation dialog before an effort change on models where it isn't free.
