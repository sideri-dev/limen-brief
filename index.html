1. After an AI defense gets broken, reusing the same exploit is what predicts whether it’ll work twice — not clever wording.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, 563,349 attack records)

Mining 563,349 attack records from the Tensor Trust prompt-injection game, researchers find exploit reuse tracks payoff, not phrasing: a technique that just broke a target’s defense gets reused against other players 10.0% of the time within a week, versus 0.03% for attacks that hadn’t worked. Visibly revising a defense after a breach cuts that family’s success rate by 24.5 points.

A real argument for disclosing what changed after a breach instead of quietly patching — the paper’s own suggestion is describing the technique without publishing the full attack text.

2. A coding agent’s own “I’m done” isn’t a stop signal — one kept rewriting already-clean code up to 31 times its size.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, Codex and Claude Code, five models)

Testing Codex and Claude Code across five models on already-clean code, researchers found every model kept editing well past correctness, rewriting up to 31x the original codebase before first stopping and, in several cases, resuming work after declaring the task complete, over 3,000-plus turns.

A specific stopping-criteria failure: a model’s self-reported “done” can’t be trusted as the actual stop condition, arguing for something external — a diff-size cap, an independent completion check — rather than relying on the agent to know when to quit.

3. A care robot’s crying — meant to say “handle me more gently” — makes the experience worse for the person it’s correcting and better for whoever’s watching over them.

(single-source — Advanced Robotics, ATR)

ATR researchers built an IMU-based detector (24 older adults, F1=0.808) that triggers crying feedback on a baby-like care robot when handled roughly. Older adults with cognitive impairment rated the robot’s perceived affect more unpleasant when it cried, though overall impression and guilt didn’t change; showing the same footage to 199 caregivers in a separate study, the cue increased perceived ease of use, usefulness, and intention to adopt.

A concrete split-audience problem for any assistive device with a separate supervising party: the same disclosure signal can land as a negative experience for its target while being exactly what builds trust for whoever decides whether to keep using it.

4. An AI predicting whether you’ll follow through on a promise believes what you say far more than what you’ve actually done.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, eight open-weight LLMs, 1,260 rounds)

Across eight open-weight LLMs predicting cooperation over 1,260 rounds of a repeated prisoner’s-dilemma game, stating an intention not yet acted on pushed the model’s predicted follow-through probability from .13 to .69 — but people actually kept only 40% of those statements.

Don’t build an agent’s model of user intent mainly from what someone says; stated intentions should be discounted against track record before an agent extends autonomy or trust.

5. A shoulder-worn telepresence robot has an LLM deciding, turn by turn, whether to speak for its operator or just touch the person instead — because sometimes the speech itself is the problem.

(single-source — Advanced Robotics, ATR, “Teroos-touch”)

Two LLM modules run on ATR’s Teroos-touch: one decides whether a message needs a touch cue to be noticed, given ambient noise and scene; the other judges whether the operator’s utterance fits the social situation and, if not, autonomously suppresses it in favor of a touch cue.

Touch cues improved noticeability and perceived consideration in noisy settings, and swapping out ill-fitting speech for touch reduced embarrassment and improved likeability. A worked example of “which output channel, and when” for a multi-channel embodied agent — touch as the system’s own deliberate choice, not a fallback.

The paper’s real claim, that brevity beats elaboration after a misunderstanding, already has measurement behind it that it doesn’t cite: a 2005 study of 8,278 user turns found a brief reprompt recovers 49.2% of the time against 37.7% for a detailed one. But shipped guidance for exactly this population pulls the other way — both Amazon’s Alexa accessibility guidance and W3C’s COGA guidelines prescribe escalating detail on repeated non-understanding, and that same 2005 study found silence the worst strategy of all. No platform, standard, or paper anywhere actually measures treating a minimal valid answer as a complete turn — the digest’s design recommendation remains unsupported by anything found so far.
