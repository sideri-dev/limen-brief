1. A 2001 Army Research Laboratory study already measured the exact “automation bias” today’s AI papers keep rediscovering by vibe — and the real finding is sharper than first reported.

(primary source — ARL-TR-2412, March 2001)

Participants predicted an automated decision aid would be right “nearly 93% of the time,” but rated a comparably-positioned human aid — another participant’s prior track record, not their own judgment — “only 27% better than chance,” about 77%. That’s a measured ~16-point overestimation of automation’s reliability versus an equivalent human input; an earlier draft of this item compared it to self-judgment instead, which the correction sharpens rather than weakens.

A usable calibration check against any interface that defaults to trusting the automated answer, from exactly the pre-LLM human-factors literature worth mining directly rather than rediscovering.

2. Personalized, AI-written counterfactual stories make distant risks feel close — and it works about as well whether or not you already like AI.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, two pre-registered RCTs, N=1,901 total)

Two pre-registered, quota-matched RCTs (science funding, N=1,007; satellite cybersecurity, N=894) tested AI-co-written personalized narratives against generic framing, finding effect sizes from 0.36 to 0.90 SDs depending on the outcome, with the AI-attitude moderation effect a small 0.10 SDs.

The rigor is unusual for this lane, and the finding matters for anyone using AI personalization to make abstract risk information actionable: the lift doesn’t depend on the reader already trusting AI.

3. Figma tried prompting a frontier model for canvas edits, decided it was “incredibly token-consumptive,” built its own fine-tuned model instead — and then had to add reinforcement learning because the fine-tuned model just copied its homework.

(single-source, vendor-primary — Figma Blog, Oct 29 2026)

Figma trained on more than 250,000 examples using Llama 3.3 70B rather than pay the token cost of frontier-model prompting for every edit. Imitation learning alone produced a model that reproduced training-set style at the expense of generalizing, so the team layered in reinforcement learning against expert-written quality criteria — a concrete rebuttal to “just fine-tune it” as a finished strategy.

Figma also names a latency boundary worth stealing directly: “an edit that takes too long can interrupt the very workflow it’s supposed to help.”

4. Nielsen Norman Group: the AI-chatbot mistake worse than escalating to a human too often is refusing to hand off someone who explicitly asked for one.

(single-source — nngroup.com, July 10 2026)

NN/g’s guidance sets an explicit order: escalating too quickly erodes trust and raises labor cost, and offering options outside a bot’s competence reads as upselling — but gatekeeping an explicit request for a human is the worse failure, full stop.

The same piece backs upfront disclosure over fine print, since users don’t read privacy policies. A clean, decision-grade escalation rule for any support or agent interface.

5. A practitioner’s prelaunch checklist for killing an AI feature before sunk cost makes that impossible: a numeric kill metric, a kill date set in advance, and exactly one person with the authority to pull the trigger.

(single-source — Tian Pan, tianpan.co, April 10 2026)

Tian Pan’s framework names the threshold concretely (“if task completion rate doesn’t improve by 15% within 90 days”), insists the kill date be set “before you’ve invested enough to trigger sunk cost psychology,” and is blunt about ownership: “if the kill decision requires consensus, it will never happen.”

The underlying pre-launch test is worth borrowing verbatim: “if you had a perfect model with zero hallucinations and instant latency, would users still want this workflow?”

Yesterday’s dossier

Full-text reading of yesterday’s #1 item (arXiv:2610.36422, the Tensor Trust prompt-injection reuse paper) corrects its framing. “Describe the technique without the full text” is not a tested finding but one of four hedged hypotheses the paper leaves to a future study, and the digest’s “argues for disclosing what changed after a breach” inverts the paper’s actual question — whether to withhold attack text from the breached party, not whether to disclose a change to anyone.

The 24.5-point headline rests on only 203 of 12,531 breach events (1.6%) and swings from −16.5 to −47.4 depending on an arbitrary matching threshold. The posture it gestures at is already CERT/CC’s 2003 Limited Disclosure practice and Cloudflare’s shipped default, so the paper measures a known tradeoff, not a new one; its substrate is also 2023-era models in a game whose designers predicted this behavior in print, and an older same-dump analysis reports a much smaller effect on a different measure.
