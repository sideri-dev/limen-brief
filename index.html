1. A CHI 2026 paper names the actual repair loop a confirmation surface needs to support, and argues against both every-step and end-only approval.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

The paper frames agent-confirmation placement as a scheduling problem balancing interruption cost against rollback cost, and a formative study (N=8) surfaced a recurring “Confirmation-Diagnosis-Correction-Redo” (CDCR) pattern — the actual shape a user’s repair takes when an agent gets something wrong mid-task. A larger within-subjects evaluation (N=48) found 81% of participants preferred intermediate confirmation over confirm-at-end, with task completion time cut 13.54%.

It’s a measured model with a named taxonomy, not another “reject per-step approval” opinion essay — worth building a monitoring/undo surface around CDCR specifically rather than a generic checkpoint.

2. People rate AI advice as more rational and trustworthy than human advice, but it doesn’t move the behavior it’s meant to change.

(single-source — arXiv preprint)

Participants judged LLM advisors significantly more rational, trustworthy, convincing — and sycophantic — than human advisors in a multi-turn conversation. Despite that preference gap, donation decisions didn’t vary across conditions at all.

This matters for how you evaluate an AI advisory feature: a lift in trust or satisfaction ratings is not evidence the feature changed the decision it was built to influence, and a feature that reads well in a survey can still be doing nothing.

3. Builder.io’s new Claude Code skill rejects freeform plans for schema-enforced components, and is explicit about where that stops working.

(single-source — vendor)

/visual-plan replaces “wall of text” agent plans with typed, scannable MDX components, on the reasoning that freeform text has “infinite entropy” — left alone, an agent wanders, omits details, or hallucinates structure that was never checked. The schema isn’t decoration, it’s enforcement: a typed field forces an explicit gap into the open where a paragraph could paper over it.

The company is equally clear about scope — this model is for autonomous, long-running execution loops, not interactive work, where a structured plan is “your only lever for control.”

4. A millisecond figure for an embodied agent’s nod: ~300ms reads as empathy, instant reads as proactive.

(single-source — Japanese-original; participant count partially unverified, tooling gap)

A Japanese HCI journal study on character-gaze response found a 300ms delay before a nod rated highest for empathy and reassurance, while an immediate, 0ms response rated highest for felt activeness/proactiveness. That’s a concrete number for a design choice usually left to intuition — nod latency is a decision about which impression you’re building, not a performance detail.

One caveat: the paper’s participant count couldn’t be recovered from the PDF in this environment (a CJK-font extraction gap, not a sourcing problem) — the millisecond figures and direction come cleanly from the paper’s own English abstract regardless.

5. Timing an agent’s emotional expression to the exact moment of shared musical tension reads as more co-present than generic expressiveness.

(single-source — Japanese-original)

In a 37-participant study, a music co-listening agent’s sigh expression at a chord-resolution moment read as relief, and pairing strong facial-expression intensity with that sigh produced a stronger sense of empathy — an effect that was more pronounced for musically experienced participants than for novices.

The design lesson generalizes past music: affect typed and timed to the structure of a shared experience reads as genuine co-presence, where generic expressiveness doesn’t, and the effect depends on how expert your user already is.

Yesterday’s dossier

MaterialVellum is a refereed UIST 2026 full paper, but two of the digest’s framing claims don’t hold up: the paper’s own text says material metaphors “complement rather than replace” other control paradigms and calls itself a technology probe three times, not a text-box replacement or a UI primitive. A text prompt survives in all four model-conditioned paths, written by the designers at build time, not the user; a third of the six demonstrated constraints never reach the model at all, and no objective measure of whether a generated image honored its constraint exists anywhere in the paper.

The material-metaphor move isn’t new either — PromptPaint (UIST 2023) did it first from a different lab, and this team’s own earlier work (Texterial, CHI 2026) is the direct ancestor. What’s worth keeping is the constraint anatomy as a checklist, plus one thing the paper itself doesn’t notice: its best-rated gesture is a placed prompt-picker with a hard mask and a reversible overlay, not a departure from prompting at all.
