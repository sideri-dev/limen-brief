1. A new ACM journal paper names “omnirelevance” — the background risk, present in every turn of talk, that anything a person says near a voice agent might get wrongly treated as a command.

A peer-reviewed study in ACM’s Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction gives a name to something every voice-interface designer has half-noticed: people talking near a voice agent manage their speech around the constant possibility that anything they say might be picked up and acted on. It’s a structural finding about the interaction itself, not a claim about any particular model, so it should outlast the usual shelf life of a capability claim.

The practical upshot: explicit addressivity cues — a clear wake behavior, gaze, push-to-talk — aren’t polish, they’re relief for a real and currently unpaid cost users carry every time they open their mouth near a live mic.

2. Graphical visualizations of an LLM’s reasoning help people judge when to trust it on verbal tasks, and actively hurt that judgment on visual tasks — the opposite of what users say they prefer.

A 204-person UIST 2026 study tested showing a visual map of an LLM’s rationale instead of text. On verbal-reasoning tasks the graphic improved correct trust calibration, even though users found it more mentally taxing and less satisfying to use; on visual-reasoning tasks it did the opposite — people liked it more and judged worse.

That mismatch between stated preference and actual accuracy argues against shipping one rationale-visualization pattern universally; the right call is to gate it by task modality.

3. Showing researchers exactly which evidence backs an AI’s edit lowered how much they trusted it — but didn’t change whether they relied on it.

Twenty-six researchers used a prototype (“PaperTrail”) that traces each AI-suggested edit to its scholarly source. It worked in the narrow sense of lowering stated trust significantly. But that lowered trust never showed up in behavior — people kept accepting the edits anyway, because actually checking them stayed the cognitively expensive option.

A provenance or citation layer can move what people say they believe without moving what they do; it needs a verification step bolted to it, not just a transparency one.

4. Anthropic’s new “mods” system gives third-party Claude Code extensions the same unrestricted machine access Claude Code itself has — no sandbox, by design.

The stated reasoning is structural: the existing hook system can’t rewrite events, draw new UI, or replace features outright, and mods need to do exactly that — sandboxing would have meant shipping a weaker extension surface.

It’s a clean, named tradeoff of capability over containment, worth studying as a precedent for how far to let third-party code reach into an agent runtime. Anthropic states “install only from sources you trust” as the actual safety boundary, not any technical one.

5. People don’t forgive AI mistakes more easily on tasks that look hard than on tasks that look easy — the difficulty framing buys nothing.

A preregistered study of 577 people confirmed the obvious half: more observed errors reduce use. But difficulty framing turned out not to matter at all — easy-task failures hurt trust just as much as hard-task ones.

That’s a clean argument for building friction and disclosure budgets around the actual observed error rate, not around how difficult a task appears to be.

The four-outlet corroboration the digest credited turns out to be one reporting chain through a single working outlet plus an aggregation tail, not four independent accounts. Meta’s only on-record statement was a decline to comment; the “correctly asked for permission” line was about separate past reports, not this incident.

The digest’s own proposed fix — replace the single toggle with per-action-class consent — is contradicted by the one preregistered test of exactly that design, where users set “ask” on most rules and then approved nearly all the overreach actions that triggered anyway. What the evidence actually supports is a scoped standing grant plus a real inhibiting delay at the moment of crossing it, not finer-grained prompting.
