1. Lane-keeping systems that say “detected” are wrong about when to escalate, and it’s measured now.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, 39,800s of real driving across 13 vehicle models)

Across 39,800 seconds of real driving (13 vehicle models, 3 manufacturers), a lane-keeping assist’s on-dash “detected / not detected” status catches only 47% of degraded-perception seconds, against 80% for an independent confidence signal — and in half the low-confidence episodes it says nothing at all. When the dash reads “detected” but confidence is actually low and the driver’s hands are off the wheel, lateral drift over the next three seconds runs about 60% higher.

The researchers’ own conclusion: staying silent while the driver isn’t in control is exactly the condition that most needs an alert — a concrete answer to when “all clear” should flip to an active warning instead of silence.

2. Always-on voice agents run out of road after “tens of minutes,” not hours.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, new FD-LHB benchmark)

A new benchmark tested five full-duplex voice models on hour-long replays and live conversation; every one eventually degrades or runs out of context — repeating itself, going silent, or producing gibberish. The cause in sliding-window architectures is “Initialization-Anchor Eviction”: the window quietly discards the first exchange that anchors the conversation.

Two training-free fixes, pinning that anchor and penalizing repetition, substantially reduce the collapse in real time without retraining. Any “always-on” duplex interface on current architectures needs an explicit session-length budget — reset, anchor-pin, or turn-taking fallback — because this failure arrives on a clock, not a vendor’s roadmap.

3. Confidence badges change what people say before they know the answer, then stop mattering.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, interviews + 1,000-person survey + 5,151-person experiment)

A mixed-methods study — 8 interviews, a 1,000-person survey, a 5,151-person experiment — finds confidence and source indicators do shift initial trust and accuracy judgments, but people’s interpretations of “AI confidence” are genuinely all over the map.

Once someone learns whether the answer was right, the indicator’s effect disappears entirely; medical queries stay skeptical regardless. That’s a real ceiling on confidence UI as error communication: it moves first impressions, not verified judgment, and can’t substitute for friction appropriate to the domain.

4. A Japanese hospital is running its own LLM on-site, and letting the agent ask its own questions beats fixed retrieval by 16 points.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, Hokkaido University Hospital)

Hokkaido University Hospital has run an open-weight, roughly 1-trillion-parameter model (Kimi K2.6) on a single 8-GPU host since September 14, serving 16 concurrent clinical users at 908 tokens/second total with no cloud round-trip.

On 180 hospital-document questions, interactive, tool-driven document access hit 97.2% accuracy against 81.3% for fixed top-k retrieval — a 15.9-point gap, tight confidence interval. For a team building a self-hosted clinical assistant to avoid cross-border data negotiation, this is both a real throughput budget and a measured answer on the retrieval-architecture question.

5. Novices correcting AI output are mostly judging fit, not catching errors.

(single-source — arXiv preprint, 145 first-year engineering students)

A field study of 145 first-year software-engineering students across 962 AI-interaction turns found 74.4% of their interventions were about whether the output fit the task, versus 16.9% about whether it was correct — and when they intervened, they mostly edited the output directly (51%) or rewrote their prompt (28%), not accepted or rejected it outright.

That argues against an oversight interface built around error detection alone: the dominant real behavior is a fitness judgment, favoring edit-in-place over binary accept/reject. The caveat is in the sample — first-year students, not expert reviewers — so this is a lead on novice oversight, not yet settled for experts.
