When a human supervises a GUI agent, the safety gain comes from the agent's own caution — not from the human noticing more.

A two-phase CHI 2026 study tested six computer-use agents against 16 dark patterns, then had 22 people supervise the strongest. Supervised participants avoided the patterns in 14 of 16 tasks, but noticed fewer of them (66% "No" when asked if they'd spotted one) than when working alone (59% "No") — the gain traces to the agent's own conservative habit of stopping when something looks off, not sharper human vigilance.

That reverses a common assumption about approval-step UI: a human-in-the-loop screen can raise safe outcomes while actual scrutiny of the manipulative element drops, changing what that screen needs to surface rather than what it can assume gets caught.

Listing more of an assistant's capabilities builds perceived competence, not the trust dimension that predicts whether people rely on it.

A randomized 270-person experiment on an embedded AI assistant varied whether it displayed one function or three, with or without a rationale. Showing three functions raised perceived multifunctionality and nothing else — no other effect survived correction, and a rationale didn't reliably raise perceived intelligence.

Anthropomorphism tracked with perceived benevolence but not integrity or ability, the traits closer to reliance. A capability-listing pattern does less trust-building work than it looks like.

Agents in a long-horizon Civilization VI benchmark follow through on their own stated near-term plans only 48–66% of the time.

CivBench, a pilot across four model families and 23 runs, found plan follow-through ranging 48.2%–65.8%, and self-monitoring worse than instructed: agents told to check victory progress every 20 turns instead checked every 30–75, missing the warning window in 7 of 20 detectable defeats. The authors call these deviations under instruction, not capability gaps.

Design argument: don't trust an agent's self-reported plan as a proxy for behavior — build an external checkpoint that doesn't depend on it remembering to look.

A head-worn assistant that keys its alerts off inferred cognitive load, not just posture, cuts alert frequency 81% and still improves outcomes.

ErgoAssist classifies posture at 81% and cognitive load at 90.2% accuracy; in a preliminary deployment, routing alerts through the load estimate cut alert frequency 81% while usability rose 43%, performance 25%, and posture correction 38%.

A quantified answer to a question that recurs across ambient/embodied design: interruption threshold should key off inferred attentional state, not raw sensed condition — measured before/after, not a demo.

Raycast is replacing flat-rate AI pricing with usage-based credits, saying it can no longer subsidize the gap.

Raycast's own post: "we've been covering the difference, but we can't subsidize it the way the big model labs can"; and "we've been sitting on features that let AI work on its own, because there was no way to account for what they cost." New tiers: Pro $10/mo (500 credits), Pro+ $20/mo (3,000), Max $50/mo (7,500).

The pricing-gates-affordance pattern stated outright — flat per-seat pricing worked for single-turn Q&A but breaks against autonomous multi-step work, and metering UI unlocks the feature set, not just monetizes it.

Yesterday's dossier

Stress-testing yesterday's #1 item (the HAI turn-taking-latency study) overturns its own headline: the smoothed-average condition loses seven of eight significant impression comparisons, and "direction-matching" never significantly beats a fixed delay on anything except losing on smoothness — the real finding is "the moving-average rule is actively bad," not "direction-matching wins."

The reported recall advantage is an unmatched word-count ratio never scored against what was actually said, with an effect size that doesn't arithmetically match its own p-value, and the direction-matching rule itself is left unspecified. What survives: the diagnosis of why the moving average fails maps onto a real shipped bug — LiveKit Agents 1.8.0's DynamicEndpointing computes the same quantity this study manipulated and never feeds it to the filter. The award is genuine (independently re-verified) and the venue has no peer review — both true at once.
